&date AD50 Philippi
&date 13 years after the crucifixion

The Jews and the god-fearers gathered on the Sabbath by the riverside.

Lydia and those of us that shared her views had come down
here for many years to take part in the sabbath-day
teaching and singing.  Some of those who didn't come thought
that the slaves who did were just using it as an opportunity
to shirk work for the day.  I can't speak for the others, but
for me it was a genuinely transformational experience.

But never as much so as when Paul was there.

Paul had insisted on taking lodgings in the town when we arrived,
in spite of the fact that I had insisted that my mistress would
love to have him come and stay with us - and also in spite of the
fact that Paul's finances were at best penurious (a state I came
to realize was neither unusual nor resented).  My companions
and I obviously went back to the house and to my mistress.
She was pleased to see us, but disappointed by Paul's absence.

``Would he not come?  Where has he gone?  Why did you not
insist on him coming?'' she demanded breathlessly before I had
a chance to offer any explanations.

``They have come, mistress,'' I responded.  ``They have taken up
lodging in the town.  I did my best to persuade him that he
should stay with us, and believe me, he could use the support,
but he point-blank refused.  And a more stubborn man never
was born.''

``And you should know,'' she responded mockingly.  It is true
that I have something of a reputation, possibly undeserved, for
being stubborn.  I prefer to think of myself as not easily 
discouraged.

``When will I see him then?'' she asked, relieved that he was at
least coming, but disappointed at the further wait.

``He will be at the riverside gathering on the Sabbath,'' I
replied.  ``He says his mission is to all mankind, but that he
must work through the established Synagogues unless and until
they reject him.''

``Then we shall be there also,'' she replied.  ``The man was
not born who can gainsay me.''

Probably true.  Certainly true in my experience, but then I am just
a mere slave in her ownership.

--

The following two days dragged by.

I had a number of urgent cases to attend to that had been `looked
after' in my absence by some local associates, but I'm not
sure that I entirely approve of their methods.  Either way,
the individuals concerned perked up just on seeing me returned
from my travels.

Lydia herself was somewhat crotchety and irritable over the two
days and kept finding fault with all manner of minor details,
many of which did not really concern her.  But since she was,
in general, such a kind and benevolent owner, most of us
simply overlooked the harsh words and minor criticisms and got
on with our work as best we could.

Finally the sabbath arrived and those of us going to the
prayer-meeting made sure we were ready and in our finest -
but nevertheless sober - clothes to attend.  It's an
interesting thing, but the Jewish rituals call for a strange
blend of respect yet quiet demeanour when contemplating God.
The brash rituals of the Greek or Roman religious celebrations
are nowhere to be seen.  I hear it is different at the temple
in Jerusalem - where animals are sacrificed and the air is
heavy with the smells of incense, but as a non-Jew I would
never be allowed closer than the Court of the Gentiles - the
outermost court around the Temple precincts.  In my
experience, as I say, it is a solemn and sober occasion,
infused with a great deal of humility and respect.

When we were all ready, we left the main house and proceeded
down the streets to the South Gate.  The gate was open, of
course; bandits only generally attack during the night and
there hasn't been a war in this part of the world in my lifetime.
You can blame the Romans for a lot that is wrong in the world,
but the Pax Romana is a great blessing.

As always, there was a throng of people in the streets.  People
are always going somewhere for some reason or another.  Apart
from the Jewish rituals by the riverside, other events were
going on - sacrifices at the temples, shows at the theatre,
probably some events on at the games field.  On top of that,
people had all sorts of reasons for being out and about, from
eating and drinking to their daily business and social meetings.

The Jews are ritually constrained in their movements on the Sabbath.
This day, they say, was set aside by their Lord (and their Law)
for rest and contemplation.  Among the other constraints, they
are not supposed to travel more than `necessary' on the Sabbath,
a somewhat vague measure that has, in recent times, come to mean
substantially less than a mile.  In consequence, many of the Jews
live near the South Gate: in order to be close to the place of
prayer come the morning.

So, as we came closer to the gate, we saw more and more people headed
our way, ready and attired for the prayer meeting.  We walked out
of the gate and down to the river bank.  Three of us spread out
the matting and my mistress and her sisters sat on it.  We
slaves kneeled behind.

When everybody had gathered, one of the elders of the Jewish
church stood up and read from their holy books.  The readings
are, of course, in Hebrew, a language which all of the `real'
Jews know but is completely foreign to us.  Having said that,
most of them seemed to know not just the language but the
entire text of their scriptures.  Once the reading was complete,
the elder sat down again and another began to expound on the
theme they had chosen: the time of exile when the followers
of God were banished from Judea and had to spend years in
Babyonia (now part of the Parthian Empire, far to the East),
until they repented of their sins and came back to the bosom
of God.

He then introduced Silas, one of Paul's travelling companions,
`a Jew of good standing from Jerusalem and Antioch', to read
the Second Reading.  Silas read from the book of the prophet
Ezekiel about the return from exile and the coming of the
Kingdom of God.

And then he turned to Paul to expound it.
