@Chapter
title=Breaking Bread

&date AD 51 Philippi
&date 18 years after the crucifixion

Paul looked up to heaven before he broke the bread.

Friday nights were `feast nights' in the Way.  All who
were members of the Way in any given locality would
meet at a house of one of the members - here in Philippi
that was always at Lydia's, which was certainly
convenient for me.  All the members would bring what food
they had and would share among all equally.  In Jerusalem,
Paul said, many members of the Way lived holding everything
in common, caring only about spreading the Word.

Here in Philippi we maintained our individual wealth,
in Lydia's case quite great; mine less so.  But when we
came together on Friday nights, the rigid social caste
of the week was left behind and we were all brothers
and sisters in Christ.

``Remember,'' intoned Paul, ``the words of our master
before he was crucified, when he ate the last meal with
his students in Jerusalem.

``Those of you who have memorized these sayings, say it
with me.

``Jesus said to them, `With all my heart I have longed
to eat this Passover with you before the time comes for
me to suffer. Believe me, I shall not eat the Passover
again until all that it means is fulfilled in the kingdom of God.'

``Then taking a cup from them he thanked God, and said,
`Take this and share it amongst yourselves, for I tell you
I shall drink no more wine until the kingdom of God comes.'

``Then he took a loaf and thanked God.

``Then he broke it and gave it to them, with these words,
`This is my body which is given for you: do this in
remembrance of me.'

``So too, he gave them a cup after supper with the words,
`This cup is the new agreement made in my own blood which
is shed for you.' ''

Paul paused and looked around.  ``This bread,'' he said,
``is broken as the Lord's body was broken.  This wine is
split as his blood was spilt.  But as you eat and drink,
think not of daily sustenance but of the spiritual
sustenance for which he gave his life.

``The new agreement was made in his blood.  He died for
us, and he rose for us, that we might, through his
atonement for our sins, be reborn in him to a new life,
to a life eternal and glorious, one with the angels.''

And he was off.  Another one of Paul's impassioned,
beautiful, vigorous proclamations of the coming of the
Kingdom of God was underway.  And it was impossible
to sit back and listen; I was completely gripped and
wrapped up in the experience.

--

One advantage of many of having the meeting in "our" house
was that it was not far after the meeting broke up that
we had to go to get to bed.  For Paul, of course, it was a
long time before he could shed his crowd of admirers,
questioners, interlocutors.

But eventually we moved away.  I didn't always wait for him;
he was perfectly capable of making it back to his own room.
But on this occasion, I had seen _it_ very clearly, while
he was in the middle of one of his stories about Jesus.

``I've figured it out,'' I told him excitedly when we were
finally alone.

``Figured what out?'' he asked.

``How we can spread the word more quickly; more efficiently,''
I replied.

He looked at me blankly.  For me, this had been a pressing
need ever since my baptism.  Clearly, for him, the idea
of spreading the word more efficiently had been blown to
the back of his mind and forgotten there.

``It came to me when you were telling that story about the
son who ran away and then came back,'' I began.  He nodded,
remembering the story only too well - one he had probably
told countless times.

``Stories like that really make the coming of the Kingdom
apparent.  You don't want to be caught in the far-flung
deserts of depraved living when the King arrives, do you?
We could publish a scroll of stories, the Jesus-stories,
along with some of his other sayings and doings, and send
copies around the empire.''

``To whom would you send them?'' he asked.  ``You see the
opposition I receive from Jews who could hear those stories
and believe.  To Gentiles, they would be just that - stories.
Why would a Gentile listen to a story like that and think
that God was real?  It would mean nothing to them unless,
like you, that had had an encounter with the Spirit of God.''

I felt a little deflated.  I had felt I was so right; no,
I knew deep in my bones that I was right.  But his logic
was unanswerable: who would pay attention?

``Do your communities in Asia have them?'' I asked.  ``We
have heard many of your sayings, and some we have learnt,
but we do not have all of them.  Maybe I should at least
write them all down so that we will have a copy of them all
when you leave us.''

``I am not sure that it is possible to collect all of them,''
he replied, ``but I cannot stop you writing the words of
God if that is your pleasure.  And if it is your mistress'
pleasure to give you the time to do so.''

``I'm sure that will not be a problem,'' I countered, knowing
well that nothing would give her greater pleasure than
to be able to help with the work.  But I knew I would have
to talk to her.

--

``What would the purpose be?'' she asked earnestly when I
raised the idea with her the following week.

``I have two ideas in view,'' I replied. ``In the first
instance, I feel sure that Paul will not be with us much
longer.  When he leaves, we will be left without a source
of inspiration, a teacher or a guide.  What will we do?

``And secondly, if there were such a scroll, we could
publish it and share it with other communities.  Initially,
communities in Christ, but later we could share it with
other communities - communities that have not yet heard
the Word.''

``A scroll to keep the words of the preacher alive,'' she
mused.  ``Much as the Jews already keep the words of their
Law and the words of the prophets.''

``Exactly,'' I agreed.  ``But these would be the living
words and stories from God's appointed redeemer.  Told
with the vision and passion that Paul brings to them.''

``How would you go about it?'' she asked and my heart leapt!
For her to ask this question, I knew she had agreed in her
heart and it was just a question of making it happen.  I
tried to leash my excitement but failed.

``Many of the stories I have heard.  I can start with those
and write them down in my own time.  Then I can spend time
with Paul as he works and listen to more stories.  And
commit those to paper too.  And, in the fulness of time,
try and put some structure to it.''

``You mean put in one of those flowery introductions so
popular in Greek prose?  `To Governor Felix, who made this
possible by not randomly executing me because I didn't bribe
him enough'?''

I smiled.  Her turn of phrase was ever amusing.  Not to mention
her sense of the ridiculous.

``No,'' I replied sweetly; and then inspiration struck.  ``I
would dedicate it to you, of course.  You are the one who
would make it possible.''

``To a woman?'' she mocked.  ``That would not be seemly, Luke.
Better to dedicate it to a slave.''

``We could use an alias,'' I improvised quickly.  You
and I would both know who was meant, but the forms would be
observed.''

``Lydius?'' she smiled, still mocking me.

``Maybe something more about your position, in the scheme
of things,'' I suggested.  ``A sponsor, an interested party,
a lover of new things.''

``Or a lover of Godly things,'' she countered.  ``Theophilus
- it has a certain ring to it.''

``And it constrasts nicely with how the Jews see us, as
God _fearers_,'' I added.

``O Theophilus,'' she declaimed, ``on account of your great
beauty and interest in things spiritual, I dedicate to you
this work on the life and sayings of Jesus, known as the
Christ, come to save the world?''

``Something like that,'' I said.  ``But that is for the future.
The most important thing is to capture the words of Paul
while he is with us.''

``I sense you feel that will not be long,'' she inferred.

``I sense that he senses it,'' I replied.  ``I don't know,
but it feels like a few months at most.''

In fact, it was less than two weeks, and I had hardly started
to gather stories from him in that time.
