@Chapter
title=Cleopas

&date AD 57 Jerusalem
&date 24 years after the resurrection

If I couldn't draw any secrets from the Tomb, I could track down Jesus'
actions after he left it.

Mary had told me about Cleopas and David, and I decided to try and
track them down.  It turned out that during the persecution David
had fled to Alexandria.  He might or might not still be there; he
might, indeed, be dead.  If I went to Alexandria, I might look for
him there; but I wasn't about to make a special trip right now.  Leaving
Paul in Caesarea for a week to come to Jerusalem was one thing;
Alexandria was a major trek.

But Cleopas had remained in Jerusalem, even during the persecution.
When I met him, it became obvious why.  Unlike many of the firebrand
prophets and apostles, this was a quietly spoken man who considered
everything before he tried to change the minds of others.  He had
probably never even come to the attention of the authorities as a
troublemaker; even if he had, he would have probably engaged the
sympathies of most judges.

He lived with a number of other members of the Way in one of their
`common houses' spread around the city.  Given what Mary had told
me, he wasn't hard to find.  When I met him, he was at his work desk
doing what he had been doing for most of his adult life: writing.
Cleopas was a scribe.

``When I was younger, I used to copy out the Law and the Prophets,''
he told me after I had introduced myself.  ``But now I do a variety
of tasks for many people.  But always I try to do things that
support the Way as best I can.  And, as I'm sure you know, all the
money that comes in is shared equally between all of us.  Many
devout Jews do not want to buy a copy of the Law from a man in
a splinter faith.''

I could see that.  While the persecution of which I'd heard had
seemed to die out in Judea - and was certainly not to be compared
to anything that Paul had suffered in Asia - under the surface
there was still a considerable tension between those who followed
God in the Way of Christ Jesus and those who did not: who
believed the the Messiah was yet to come and the end times were
not yet here.

``Paul did not do us any favours by coming to Jerusalem and stirring
the pot again,'' he noted, but without any obvious acrimony: a
statement of fact rather than opinion.  ``His brand of the Way,
with its divergence from following the Law, inflames passions in
a fashion we haven't seen since the great persecutions.  They
cannot trust him, undermining the Law and mixing in idolatrous
beliefs about the afterlife.''

I didn't really feel qualified to answer this.  I was, of course,
one of the `Gentile hangers-on' who didn't accept or obey the Law
to whom he was obliquely objecting.  Or noting others' objections to.
And if Paul's trial did lead to a stirring-up of old passions,
this harmless man might be caught up in a fresh persecution.  It
was understandable that he would be concerned.  But, at the same
time, Paul had an obligation - an obligation of the Spirit, no
less - to preach the Word as he had received it, didn't he?

``He may well do,'' Cleopas accepted when I put this to him.  ``And
I, personally, have no objection to him doing that in Asia or Europe.
But when he comes here, where the old ways are strongest, and
stirs up a hornets' nest, all of us suffer.  Did he need to come here?
I'd heard he was appointed Apostle to the Diaspora.  Whatever
Jerusalem may be in these times, it is _not_ part of the Diaspora.''

That I could not disagree with.

--

Eventually, I managed to steer the conversation where I wanted it to go.

``The day of the resurrection?  Of course, I remember it as clearly
as if it were yesterday.

``It was on the afternoon of the first day of the week - the day that
Jesus rose from the dead, but of course we didn't know that at the time.
David and I were heading back from the City having celebrated the Passover
and the Sabbath and were going home to Emmaus - where we used to live in
those days.

``It's about seven miles or so from here, but down and then up, so it ends
up taking about half a day, particularly if the weather is hot - or rainy.
And as we set off fairly early in the morning, there was only really
one thing on our minds - what had been happening over the past few days.

``You have to understand, I was not one of Jesus' followers at that point.
I'd heard about him - as I'd heard about John by the Jordan - but I hadn't
gone out to see him preach.  I was happy doing what I was doing and 
didn't see any need to upset the apple cart for someone who was probably
just a rabble-rouser.

``And then he came to Jerusalem for Passover.  Being only a few miles away,
I would come up to Jerusalem every Passover, usually just for a couple of
days.  On this occasion, with Passover being the day before the Sabbath, I
had to stay an extra day, so we arrived late on the Wednesday.

``The City was in uproar.

``Emmaus is a fairly sleepy town and while I'd heard about Jesus and
had heard that he was planning on being in Jerusalem for the Passover,
we'd not heard anything about the events in Jericho, where he managed
to offend all the priests, let alone the near-riot that had followed
his entrance into Jerusalem.

``By the time we arrived on the Wednesday, it seemed that almost nobody
was talking about anything else.  I'd thought that if he were to preach
in the Temple grounds, I'd go and listen - he might have something
interesting to say - but when I arrived on the Thursday morning the
courts were packed and just about the only people that were talking
were the Temple lawyers.  They were asking him difficult points of
law and trying to trip him up on all kinds of details.  And the way
he evaded them!  It was a sight to see.

``Clearly, he knew the Law backwards.  Whenever they asked him a specific
question that involved a phrase such as `What is written in the Law?' or
`How do you read Moses?' he gave them a word-for-word repetition of the
clause or commandment they were looking for.

``And it was the same for the prophets.  When someone asked him about the
future of Jerusalem, he responded by citing `the judgment of Jeremiah on
the city' and then quoted the relevant passages in full!  I tell you,
you might not have liked the man, or agreed with him, but he knew all
the traditions of the people!

``But where he really set himself apart from any fool with a good memory
was when they asked him some of the questions that they come up with in
doctrinal school - or somewhere.

``I'll give you one example.  They asked him about marriage.  The Law says
that if a woman marries a man, but he dies with her still barren, one of his
brothers should marry her and bring up children `but in his brother's name'.
Now, they suggested, imagine a whole slew of brothers, each of whom marries
the same woman in turn and then dies, and at the end of the day, they are all
dead and she is still without child.  If you believe in the resurrection,
they say, whose wife will she be?

``I remember the look on Jesus face.  It wasn't quite contempt, but it was
a look of supreme disconcern for the hypothetical woman's fate.  `What is
marriage', he asked rhetorically.  `Is it not a material institution for
the proper raising of children?  But in the resurrection, who can be a
child, `for they shall all know me even as I know them'.  So what is the
need of marriage?  Each shall be their own individual, and not bound to
another.  They do not die, they do not give birth, they do not marry.
Your question reveals your ignorance of much, not just the resurrection.'

``More than one of them were disconcerted at this answer and ceased from
trying to entrap him.  Then he turned the tables on them.

`` `Let me ask you this,' he said, 'on the subject of marriage.  If a man
is married, is it lawful for him to commit adultery with another woman
and still serve as a priest?'

``And he walked forward and looked one of the doctors of the law in eye
and repeated `Well?  Is it?'.  The man turned purple, stood up and fled
from the Temple, pushing others out of his way, in his hurry to leave.

`` `Beware of false lawyers!' he proclaimed to the people.  `They may
enjoy walking around in long robes.  They may love people bowing to
them in public.  They enjoy the privileges and honours.  But are they
better men?  I doubt it.  Look not on the outward appearance!  It is
what is on the inside that will be judged on Judgment day!' ''

--

Cleopas had remained in Jerusalem for the Passover and the day of 
the crucifixion; although he had not been present at any of the key events
involving Jesus, he had heard about them and had a fair knowledge of all
that had happened.  By the time he left Jerusalem, there was almost
nothing else he wanted to talk about.

``As we walked down to the Kidron, we were discussing the things we'd
heard him say.  And it seemed to us that he had done nothing to commit
blasphemy, which is what they appeared to have accused him of.

``I was convinced that he was a genuinely good man.  I wouldn't have said
at that moment that he _was_ the Messiah, but I don't think I would
have been able to deny it either.

``But surely if he had been the Messiah, God would not have permitted
him to die.  And I was just saying this when we came up to a man sitting
on a rock by the wayside.  We stopped and asked if he needed anything -
water, or food.

`` `You would share with me?' he asked.

`` `Gladly,' I replied.  `We have plenty and to spare for this walk;
we are only going to Emmaus.'

`` `Then I will take what I value most,' he replied. `Your company.'

``And we walked the rest of the way together.

`` `You were talking most earnestly when you came along,' he offered.  `What
were you discussing?'

`` `About Jesus and his death.'

`` `Who is that?  What happened?'

`` `How can you have been in Jerusalem and not heard all about the Jesus
business.'

`` `I may have heard something,' he said, cryptically.  `But why don't you
tell me all about it, since you clearly know so much.'

`` `There was a man, called Jesus, who had come from Nazareth.  He had
been preaching for years in the hill towns around Galilee.  But a few weeks
ago he caused a stir by showing up the priests in Jericho and preferring the
company of tax collectors and prostitutes.  And then he came to Jerusalem
for the passover.  We heard him on Thursday.'

`` `And what did he have to say?  What was his preaching?'

`` `He was very strong on the Law and Prophets.  Never have I heard anyone
speak so strongly, so clearly and so effortlessly - as if he had truly
internalized them, not just memorized them.

`` `And his opinions on other matters - on difficult questions surrounding
the law and the contrary views - he cut through them with the grace and
wisdom of a Hillel.  Often it seemed that he was quoting Hillel's judgments -
it would not be too surprising if he had learnt those as well - but often
he came up with new, clear and riveting opinions of his own.

`` `And he was so unafraid of the authorities.  He even chased one of the
lawyers from the Temple, accusing him of adultery.  On what grounds, I do
not know, except that the lawyer's own reaction convicted him.

`` `But then we heard that he was betrayed by one of his own and handed 
over to the authorities for a closed trial; after which he was handed to
the Roman Procurator and sentenced to death.

`` `We didn't see any of that firsthand, we were with family at the time,
still celebrating the Passover.

`` `And then this morning, just before we left, we heard a rumour that
the grave in which he had been placed was empty!  So many oddities,
so hard to explain.

`` `But the question that burns in my soul is `Was he the Messiah?' and,
if he was, `Why would God let him die?'

`` `Why is that the question that burns in your soul?' the stranger asked.
`Would a better question not be, how can I repent and be certain of
my place in the judgment?'

``I had to accept that that was a better question, but it seemed
somehow less immediate.  If this man had seen what I had seen, he might
have understood.

``But then he starting talking about the Messiah, and pointing out
that in the Law, and in the Prophets, everything came together to
make it clear that the life of the Messiah would not be an easy one.

`` `And Zechariah says that the false shepherd must be killed by
his own parents,' he was saying as we walked through the gates of
Emmaus shortly after midday.

`` `And only after death can the judgment be delivered,' he said.
`Until death, all men are alike, as with weeds growing alongside
the grain.  After death, it is possible to judge a man's life as
a whole and decide if he should be cast into the fire or saved
through the resurrection.

`` `And how could it be otherwise for the Messiah?  Surely he
needed to die in order that he could be judged?'

``And at this point we were in the village, and I asked him if
he was coming here or elsewhere and he answered very cryptically,
`Where I go is my business, but your business is also mine' so
I invited him to come with us and share a light meal.

``And we were there at the table when it happened.  He took a loaf
of bread and said `Let this loaf be the symbol of knowledge.  And
Father, when I break it, let their eyes be opened.  And then he
broke apart the loaf, and as he passed some bread to me my eyes
were opened and I recognized him.  It was Jesus.  I opened my
mouth to greet him ... and he passed from my sight.  I don't know,
to this day, if he left normally and I just passed out, or if he
vanished.

``David and I looked at each other and knew that the rumour was
true.  Jesus had defied death and risen from the grave.

``We immediately stood up and hurried back to the city.  We sought
out his disciples and told them what we had seen and heard.

``They were all in consternation already, with so many different
stories and rumours going on.  And nobody seemed to know the truth.

``And then, while we were all standing there, he appeared again.

`` `Why are you so slow to believe the thing that had to happen?
I tried to tell you what would happen in Jerusalem, but you were
all so fearful you could not believe.'

``And we all fell down and worshipped him as the risen Christ.''
