@Chapter
title=Zaccheus

&date AD57 Jerusalem
&date 24 years after the crucifixion

My list was growing.

Mary had pointed me to a number of other people I wanted to talk to,
especially those who had been there on `the critical day'.  While I
had heard Paul preach, been baptised and received my portion of the
Holy Spirit, I still had not physically met Jesus or had an out-of-body
experience directly with him - in the way Paul had.  To meet such
people inspired me and filled me with awe; somehow they seemed more
closely connected to the Way than I was.  I wanted to meet them, hear
their stories and, increasingly, _record_ their stories.

From the time I had first starting collecting and recording Paul's
stories of Jesus, the idea of a complete narrative of this remarkable
passage of events had been growing.  But now with Paul trapped in
Caesarea and unable to continue his direct preaching, the written word
seemed ever more important.  Not a week went by without messages arriving
from one church or another on some matter of doctrine, discipline or
how to interact with the outside world and Paul was forever
dictating responses, shepherding his various flocks in the Way.

But much as I wanted to meet Cleopas and hear of his walk with Jesus
on the day of the resurrection, my next task was to follow up with the
next lead that Philip had given me, an interesting man named Zaccheus.

Jesus had offended the establishment by any number of actions that he
had taken, but one of the most serious was by failing to recognize
the holiness of the established order and their superiority in this
regard to the lower orders - in particular prostitutes such as Mary
of Magdala and tax-collectors such as Zaccheus.

It was all but a given in Jesus' time that any cooperation with the
Romans was betraying the people of Judea.  And nothing raised more
hackles than the taxation system.  Not only were the taxes going to
pay the oppressors to continue oppressing the people, but the way in
which the taxes were collected - with the _publicani_, the tax
collectors bidding for a certain sum from the Roman treasury and then
actually collecting more than that to ensure they collected a
substantial profit, and then enforcing that collection with the assistance
of the selfsame Roman soldiers - was not only a violation of Jewish
law, but caused direct resentment.

Zaccheus had been a tax collector, and one of the more notorious
ones, extorting the people of Jericho whose taxes he was responsible
for collecting and making a significant profit while ruining many
reputable locals.

When Jesus had come to Jericho - shortly before his final entry into
Jerusalem - he had been greeted with wild cheering in the streets.
By this time, he had become famous and everybody wanted to see him.
When the leaders of the local synagogues had heard he was coming, they
prepared a delegation to meet him, but Jesus had apparently ignored
this delegation and chosen to meet with Zaccheus instead.  Philip had
hinted that the story was deeper than that, and went ot Jesus' declared
mission to rescue the lost sheep of Israel, and I wanted to hear his
story first hand.

Philip had given me a letter of introduction and I had sent a messenger to
arrange a convenient time for the meeting, so that is where I went next.

After completing the customary formalities, I asked after his story.

``You have to understand that, by this time, the word was that Jesus
was going to Jerusalem in order to claim the kingdom of David his
ancestor.

``And the assumption was, that the moment he did that, the country would
rise up and the Romans would be expelled.  And obviously, that would not
be good for anybody who was found to be collaborating with the Romans.  I
naturally assumed that if Jesus were to rise up and throw off the Roman
yoke, I would be killed in the revolution.

``Naturally, I wanted to see this man for myself: was he likely to be
a revolutionary?  Was he somebody with whom I could do a deal?  Should I
stop his planned revolution by plotting against him?

``It almost seems unreal thinking about it now.  What was I thinking?
I can hardly imagine.  Probably you're used to Paul talking that way
about his transformational experience - how can you relate to the
person you were before that kind of experience?  It changes you so
fundamentally that even trying to imagine yourself back there takes
all your creativity.  How, you ask yourself, could anybody have been
so ignorant and foolish?

``I obviously couldn't join the delegation of dignitaries; they were
all holy people who had rank and prestige in the local community.  On
the contrary, I was hated and despised by all, even the poor.  Or to
be more precise, _especially_ the poor, since many of them were poor
precisely because of me.

``Likewise, I couldn't really just mingle with the crowds, because
they largely hated me.

``But outside the windows of my house, there was a large tree - a 
sycamore, I think it was - and I was able to climb out into it.  From
there, I could look down on the crowds and the procession as it came
into the town, and the big meeting as this would-be-King met with the
high and mighty of Jericho.

``But it never happened.  We had all imagined a well-ordered procession
of pomp and glory coming into the city - the way the Greeks or Romans
would do it.  Come to that, probably the way the Maccabees would do it
if they were around today.  Certainly the way Herod would do it.

``Instead of that, a group of travellers came in through the city gates,
almost surprised and apologetic that they were causing us trouble by
being out in the streets on a hot day.

``You could tell instantly which one was Jesus, and he defied expectations
as well.  I'd been out to see John preaching, and he was all fire-and-brimstone,
wild-eyed; a true doom-sayer.

``But Jesus wasn't like that.  He was very calm, very self-contained, but
stood out.  You could not mistake him for anybody else.

``And he walked calmly down the street, acknowledging the crowd, but not
really part of it, and certainly not playing the hero role they expected.

``And he walked up to the delegation and saw them, but ignored them and kept
on walking.  Somehow he seemed to walk _through_ them, as if they were not
there, but somehow without seeming to be rudely dismissing them.  Almost as
if there were irrelevant to what he was about.

``And he walked down the street to a certain sycamore tree, as if it were
the most interesting thing in Jericho.  And he stood underneath its shady
branches and stopped.  And only then did he look up, and he looked straight
up and me and gazed straight into my eyes ... and spoke to me.  Me!!  There
were all these other people there - all of them of more significance than
me - but he stopped by me, looked at me and spoke to me.

`` `Zaccheus' - yes, he called me by name - `don't think that you every
act and thought is not seen by God who creates everything.  Come down, man,
come down.  Today it is ordained that I should stay at your house, for I am
called to save the lost sheep of Israel.

`` `Consider this if you will.  Imagine a man of high rank, a Herod maybe,
who sails off to Rome to have a tetrachy foisted upon him.  Do the people
want that?  Of course not!  The mob want to govern themselves.  So they
send a delegation to Caesar as well, saying we do not want this man to be
our Ethnarch.  Whatever.

`` `In his absence, the man delegates the authority he already has.  To this
man he says, `do this', and to another `do that'.

`` `And when he returns, he examines them and judges them according to
what they have.  And some will have extorted and some will have robbed.
Some will have ruled wisely, and some will have been popular with the 
people.

`` `And the ruler, when he returns, will reward those who have done good,
and will punish those who have done evil.

`` `And each of you should ask, where will you be on that day?  Will you
be counted among the good or the evil?  Will you be rewarded or sentenced
to death; for you know well that those who will have opposed him having
the kingdom he will put to death.

`` `My job is not to judge, or to sentence, or to put to death.  I have
one task and that one task is to rescue those who are doing evil and to
put their feet on the right path.'

``And he looked up at me again.  And with compassion that could melt ice,
he said, `Come down, Zaccheus.  It is you I have come to save from the
judgment.'

``And I came down.''

--

``I brought him into my house and he looked around at all the nice
things that I had - the things that I had acquired through exploitation
and deceit.  And he looked at me and I will never forget what he said
next.

`` `Never have I seen so clear a picture of the weight of sin on a man's
soul.'

``And I burst into tears.  He was right; he had not come to judge me.  I
was the judge.  He had just opened my eyes to my sin.  And all around
me was, as he said, the evidence of that sin.  I had been so proud of
all the possessions I had acquired and in the blink of an eye he had
made it all worthless - indeed, more than worthless.

``And before I could say anything; before I could really even know how
I felt myself, he had grasped the change that had come over me, and it
was so clear to him.

`` `Rejoice, Zaccheus, because right now you are saved!  You are a
descendant of Abraham, and today you have reclaimed that status!

`` `Worry not about what you have done or the evil you have accomplished!
You know the Law, you know what God demands of you, and you know the
evil you have committed.  You are granted a long life that you may
put that right.'

``And since then I have done the best I can to cure the evils I caused.''
