@Chapter
title=Lydia

&date AD 50 Philippi
&date Thirteen years after the crucifixion

For a while after Paul ceased preaching, there was a stunned quiet.

While he had been in full flow, there had been a bevy
of questions, comments and a certain amount of heckling.
But as he reached his peroration, the crowd realized that
not only was he deadly earnest, but that he might even
be right.  The enormity of that dented the self-confidence
of even the holiest among us - a title I would certainly
not have claimed for myself!  And that, in turn, caused a
gradual hush to settle on the crowd: a hush that was lost
in Paul's booming, strident tones while he continued, but
then shocked with its depth when he suddenly ceased talking.

After the readings and the speeches, the normal flow of events
led to more singing of Psalms, composed by a poet-King of the
Jews called David.  Eventually, order restored itself, the
Psalms were sung and the meeting, formally, came to a close.

Some wandered off, returning to the homes for quiet contemplation
for the rest of the Sabbath, but for most of us, such a
course of action was unthinkable.  I had the advantage
of not only having heard Paul preach before, but of having
spent the past fortnight in his company.  For others, the
ideas he preached were not just novel, they were _revolutionary_.
And immediate.  This was a man who was _literally_ saying that
the end of days will come in your lifetime and the number of days
left to prepare for that is very limited.

Faced with that, you really only have two choices: to dismiss
the man as a lunatic or a fraud, or to earnestly consider how
to spend every remaining minute of your life.

For myself, I could never have dismissed Paul as a fraud:
he was simply too intense, too wrapped up in what he was
preaching.  I could see him as a lunatic, but as many as
I've met in my life - and as a doctor, I see a fair few,
most of them incurable - none have been half as lucid
either in daily life or in their rantings.  No, while he might
be a lunatic, he was a different kind of lunatic to any
I'd previously met.

And that only really left one possibility: as uncomfortable
as it might be, Paul genuinely believed, for very good
reasons, that the end of days were near, if not upon us,
and that the immediate consequence of a failure to repent
and put your life in order would be to be eternally cast
outside of God's final plan for the human race: to be
the child eternally cast off by their parents.

As in Troas, small groups, mainly men, started to gather
around Paul and push and probe with questions: what was
the authority for his claims? Had he met this Jesus?
Did the Sanhedrin approve his mission?  What actual proofs
could he offer that he was telling the truth?

Lydia and most of our party waited patiently in our space.  The day
was sunny and warm, and there was no pressing need to be
elsewhere.  Some of the workers and slaves excused themselves
and went back to get on with their various tasks.

Slowly the sun crept down in the sky until dusk was
approaching.  Paul dealt with all of his questioners and
seemed never to tire of preaching, disputing, philosophizing,
helping.  Always and ever putting the case that God had
offered infallible proof of his love and care, not only
by the outstanding act of raising Jesus from the dead, but
through the gift of the holy spirit to all those who
subsequently believed because of that.  That gift,
he promised and repeated, was available to any today who
would soften their hearts, believe, repent and _hear
the gospel_ - actually _listen_ to the words God spoke,
not in the _hearing_ of men but in their _hearts_.

The last of the doubters gone, still disputing in their
little groups, Paul came over to our party and greeted
me as a fellow-traveller.  And then greeted Lydia.

``Good Lady, you must be the Lydia of whom I have heard so
much from your devoted servant, Luke.  Tell me, how
may I be of assistance to you today?''

``You already have,'' she replied, ``merely by being here.
We are by the river; what impedes me from being baptised
this day?''

``Are you ready, good lady, to take that step with the
fulness of your heart and spirit?''

At her affirmative, he continued: ``then nothing, good
lady is between you and God.  Let us proceed to the
river.''

We walked down to the river almost in a procession: Paul,
my mistress Lydia, and a group of seven of us slaves
who felt the spirit move us to be baptised.

When we reached the river, Paul called us to attention
and made it clear that this was no easy step.

``I need to repeat that this is not a simple outward
ritual; this is merely a physical sign of the inward
renewal in your hearts.

``To go forward from here is a lifetime commitment.  Up
until this point, you have lived your life as you have chosen
and the consequences have been as they have been, but they
have been yours alone.

``Jesus died for us; Jesus rose for us; from this day
forward, everything you do you do in Christ.  By this
act of baptism, you are, as it were, born anew: not
born in the ordinary generation of men, but born of the
spirit, with God as your father and Jesus as your
brother.  You will all become brothers and sisters in
Christ who died for us.

``If any of you are unready for this step, now is the
last minute to say so.  When you claim the baptism
and rebirth that is in Christ, you will no longer
be ignorant as other Gentiles are, but you will be
adopted into the family of God himself.  Think
carefully!  Is this what you want, or do you wish to
back away?''

He paused, and gazed at each of us in turn.  I cannot
speak for the others, but I felt rent through.  I was
ready to turn tail and run away; but I had glimpsed
in small measure the truth that was on offer here; I
had to know more.

In the end, two of my fellow slaves turned and left;
not in shame or disgrace, but in recognition that they
were not ready.  None jeered or rejected them; some
were ready, others not.

``If you are ready, then it is time.  Remove your outer
layers of clothing and step down into the river.
Open your heart to receive the blessing that comes
from joining the brotherhood of Christ.''

Slowly we undressed and walked along the bank and down
into the river.  I know from long experience that the
waters there are cold even in the height of summer,
but on that occasion I did not even notice.

As Paul reached up and poured water over me and said
the words of blessing, I felt transformed - just as
he had promised.  From the pit of my stomach I felt a
sensation welling up and overcoming me.  The force of
it was so strong I nearly fainted right there in
the water.

I looked around at the others and they seemed
transformed: I nearly didn't recognize any of them!
Each of them was glowing as if a lamp had been placed
inside them, and on their faces an expression of
peace and joy, what I think might be called _rapture_.
I knew, right there and then, that I would never be
the same again.  And that, whatever I did, I could
never repay Paul for this priceless gift.  But I
knew I would never stop trying.

--

``But I insist.''

After returning to the river bank and dressing,
Lydia had continued with her original plan to
try and convince Paul to come and live with us.
For whatever reasons, he felt that was not what
he wanted to do.  It had almost become a battle
of wills at this point.  I don't know how she
did it; I felt incapable of anything, just
surrendered to the amazing feeling of the rebirth.

Paul continued to insist that he could find work
and afford lodging and did not want to be beholden
to anybody for anything - except Christ.  Feeling as
I did, I could understand his desire to not let
anything come between him and his mission.  But
still Lydia persisted.

``I have work; plenty of it.  Not all of it is
that which a Jew would consider unclean.  It would
be possible for you to work for me and lodge with
me and to pay for your lodging out of the wages
I would pay you for your work.

``And you would still be free to do your real work.
I would not require you to work on the Sabbath,
and you could be here preaching.  I, myself, would
come and listen and break bread with you.''

Paul sighed.

``You make it impossible for me to refuse, your
ladyship.  The ability to work for my living and
still to keep on the mission on which I know I
have been sent is all I can ask.  And, as you say,
I can lodge with you as easily as anywhere else,
especially if I am going to work for you.  Just,
I pray, give me no special favours or dispensation
that you do not give your other workers.''

``That is easily done,'' she replied.  ``Let
us consider that a bargain struck.  And now let
us return to my house that we may be ready to
start work in the morning.''

``In the morning,'' said Paul, ``early we must
break bread in commemaration of the risen Christ,
who rose from the dead early on the first day of
the week and was seen in the Garden by Mary.''

``Let us break bread early,'' agreed Lydia. ``Those
of us that are in the brotherhood.  And then let
us work God's work willingly until dusk.''

``Agreed,'' said Paul.

And we went home.  Just not the same people that
had left in the morning.
