@Chapter
title=Troas

&date AD 56 Troas
&date 23 years after the crucifixion

Paul had been away for six years.

And now he was back.  Well, in a manner of speaking.

In the past few years, he had travelled around Greece and
spent a year or more in Corinth, before turning around
and sailing back to Antioch in Syria.

We only heard from or about him occasionally, usually
from friendly merchants who had seen him and spoken to
him and brought us news.  We received a couple of highly
prized letters from him and his associates, and Timothy
briefly passed through on his way to Asia.  We put him
up for two nights and then he was off, careful not to
cause a disturbance and anxious not to be recognized.

Then last year we had had a terse note from him,
explaining that he was collecting money for famine
relief in Judea, which had been particularly badly hit
by the recent string of droughts.  Obviously, we were
delighted to collect money from our surplus to assist,
and understood that he would be coming shortly to collect
it.

But then his plans changed again and he asked us to meet him
at Troas, the very place where I had first met him.
Lydia had prepared to leave at once, and I had prepared
my begging story as to why she couldn't manage without
me.

But I never had the opportunity to use it.

``You will be coming with me to Miletus,'' she said
imperiously.  ``Bring everything you would need for
a long trip.  I sense that you will not be returning
any time soon.''

Cryptic words to be sure, but not worth analysing too
closely in the frantic relief of realizing that I was
not going to have to get down on my knees and beg to
be allowed to go with her.  It was all I could do to
stop myself from screaming out loud.

We had spent the passover in Philippi, preparing for our
trip, gathering up the collection money we had raised
and making sure everything else was able to carry on
without us.

Then, once the passover was behind us and we had
celebrated the rebirth of Christ on the following Sunday,
we had set sail for Troas.  A short, simple journey
delivered us to the rendezvous after five days and
we were reunited with Paul, Silas and Timothy with
great rejoicing.

We had spent the week in Troas, and Paul's plan from
there was to go overland to Assos and then on to Mitylene
and from there to hop the coast to various places he had
preached in the past on his way to Caesarea and Jerusalem.

But before leaving Troas at the end of the Sabbath, he
said he would preach one more time to the faithful and
we would break bread together in commemoration of the
life and resurrection of Jesus.  Since we were going to
sail to Assos and pick him up from there, we would not
be leaving until the following day.

--

Paul can certainly talk.  I'll say that much.

I'd forgotten how much he could talk and how wrapped up
in his own arguments he could become when he found his
flow.  And he'd been away so long that he obviously
felt he had so much to tell us.  And the experiences
he had had had obviously changed him.  Much of that was
in his preaching that night, but much of it was not.

We'd rented the lecture hall for the night, and people
had come from all over to hear Paul speak.  So, even
though the hall was huge, it was filled to capacity
with people crowding into the upper lofts.

I'd first seen the young man - Eutychus was his name -
sitting in the front row of one of the upper lofts when
he started to become drowsy around midnight.  I was
concerned, not so much that he would fall, as that
he might disturb the others around him.

As Paul announced that we would break bread, a great
shout of `Amen' went up and jerked the boy awake with
a start.  As he started, he lurched forward and slithered
over the railing.  The men on either side of him tried
to pull him back but could not hold their grip and
he fell to the ground below; there, a group of brothers
tried to catch him, but he evaded their best efforts
and landed heavily on the floor, winded.

As a doctor, I naturally rushed over to him as quickly
as I could; the situation looked grave, but not
necessarily fatal.  Quick as I was though, Paul was
quicker and seemed almost to translate himself across
the floor.  He bent over the young man and called
out to God for mercy.  That mercy came, and by the
time I arrived on the scene and took his pulse, he
was already in much better shape than anybody who had
just fallen from the third loft of a lecture hall had
a right to expect.

I was enjoying listening to Paul preach, and certainly
wanted to be there for the breaking of the bread, but
I knew my place.  Quickly organizing a few of the brothers
around me, we improvised a litter and carried Eutychus to
one of the anterooms off the main hall.

I gave him some water and sat by his side for a long while.
He was breathing normally and seemed to be able to move
but shortly dropped off into a sleep that seemed fairly
natural - not all that surprising given that he had
found himself in this predicament on account of falling
asleep during Paul's raucous preaching!

From the anteroom, I could still hear some of what
Paul was saying, although not at all clearly.  His
voice carried well, but lost definition through the
stone walls of the hall.  Outside, it was just starting
to become light when Eutychus sat up suddenly and
looked around.

``What happened?'' he asked.  ``I remember ... but was
it a dream?  How did I end up here?''

``No,'' I assured him, ``it was not a dream.  You nodded
off while Paul was preaching and then woke up too
suddenly when everybody acclaimed the rising of the
Christ.  You slipped and fell ...''

``But...,'' he said.  ``I was so high up; I couldn't
have survived that.''

``I don't know,'' I replied, ``other than that you
clearly did.  Paul came and prayed for God's mercy
on you; by the time I arrived you seemed remarkably
fit for a man who'd just fallen.  I'd say that you
are an incredibly lucky man.''

As we spoke a hubbub arose outside and I realized that
Paul had finished preaching.  He was now working his
way through the crowd and leaving the building with
Silas and Timothy who were going to Assos overland
with him.  As usual, a crowd of people were walking
with him, trying to say something, ask something,
take in some of his presence.  I knew how they felt;
I'd been there myself.

``Do you need a hand up?'' I asked Eutychus; he seemed
fine now and probably needed to get back home to be
ready for his day's work.

``No, I think I'm fine,'' he replied.  ``But thank you
for all your help.  I'm Eutychus, what's your name?''

``I'm Luke,'' I said.  ``I first met Paul here six
years ago, and it's strange to be back.  Was this your
first time?''

``It was my first time hearing Paul,'' said Eutychus.
``Although I've heard a lot about him.  I've been
a Brother of the Way for two or three years now.
The first brothers here were all converted by Paul - 
probably around the same time you met him.
I'm disappointed I fell asleep.  I've heard
so much about him and what he was saying was
fascinating, but he did go on, didn't he?  I thought
we'd be out of here a lot sooner than this.''

He stood up and we walked out into the crush.  It
wasn't long before someone recognized him and called
his name.

``Eutychus!  We were so worried about you.  I saw
you fall and tried to grab you, but there was nothing
that we could do!''

At this, some of the others turned around and came
towards us, recognizing the man who had fallen in
their midst.  Soon we were the centre of a second
eddying whirlpool in that crowd.

``A miracle of our own!  Not quite a resurrection from
the dead, but a man who should have died, didn't!''

Paul was long gone before I had the opportunity to
catch up with him.

When we reached Samos, Paul had another vision from
God.  He refused to speak of it to us privately, but
said we should send for those brothers who could come
from Ephesus and the surrounding areas and they
should meet us at Meelitus and he would address us all
there.

The mystery and drama added a sense of tension to the final
leg of the journey to Miletus.

He had decided to end with the coast-hopping and was
now going to set sail directly for Caesarea.  He had
taken a Nazirite vow of abstention and wished to
make it to Jerusalem in time to discharge it by Pentecost.

And then, when everybody was gathered, he addressed us
and it was clear what had changed.
