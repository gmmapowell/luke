15. Arrested

AD56 Jerusalem

23 years after the crucifixion

Now that we were on dry land and the risks of a sea voyage were behind us, the journey time was more predictable and Paul was calmer, knowing that come Pentecost he would be in the Temple. What would happen there, nobody could be quite sure, but there was general agreement that, humanly at least, it would not be a pleasant experience.

And when I say, general, I mean everybody was in agreement.

Philip, the deacon with whom we were staying, had four grown daughters. Each of them had the gift of prophesy but, as we had observed in Tyre, when you are heading straight into danger, prophesy feels like anything but a gift.

And then, the day before we left, a man called Agabus came up from the back-country beyond Jordan to see Paul; wanted to see him by name. Agabus was not a brother of the Way; he was a Jew, and a prophet among the Jews. What did he want with Paul? Was it a trap; one of the `leaders of the Jews' sent to entrap Paul into saying or doing something that he shouldn't. But Paul was confident this was a true prophet, a man sent from God, and such men could not be suborned by human will. Eventually we conceded to Paul's wishes and let the man in.

He was the perfect stereotype of the old-testament prophet, like others before him such as John the Baptist: wild-eyed, wild-haired, unkempt and looking as if he were hunted. He came in, and, without so much as a pause to ask which of us was Paul, walked straight up to him and demanded he give him his tunic. Paul, in shock, removed it and handed it over. Agabus promptly sat down on the floor and started to truss himself with it in the same way that pigs are tied - except nobody in Judea keeps pigs, judging them to be unclean animals.

We all stood there amazed at this performance. We still didn't even know who this man was, but he clearly knew - or otherwise recognized - Paul and was proceeding to embarrass him in some way or other.

``Amen, Amen, Hear ye the words of the Lord,'' he suddenly cried out, his eyes rolling up in his head leaving the whites exposed.

``Thus says the Lord of Israel, the man who owns this tunic will come to be hog-tied, even as I am hog-tied with his tunic. That man will be trussed up and taken captive by the leaders of the people in Jerusalem, and then they will hand him over to the Gentiles for punishment! Woe unto him, I say, who dares to go to Jerusalem!''

The silence was so profound you could have heard an owl ruffling its feathers. Agabus sat on the floor, tied hand and foot in Paul's tunic while Paul stood there bare-chested just staring at him.

Philip was the first to start moving again. He did not move to address or reprove Agabus, but seemed to take his prophecy to heart. ``Please, Paul, reconsider. There is no hope for you if you go up to Jerusalem. Now is the last chance to change your mind. Sail away. From here you can go anywhere in the Empire! You've always said you want to take the word to Rome, Spain and the Gauls! Go now! Do it and live! Spread the word while there is still time!''

Once Philip had said his piece, we all joined in, begging and pleading with him to abjure his journey to Jerusalem and continue doing the work of God in the open fields of the West. But he was adamant.

``What is the meaning of all this? We have been through this again and again! What difference does it make what I want? What difference does it make what any of you think is humanly a good idea? Does it matter if Paul lives or dies? No, it matters how Paul lives and dies. If I am to run away, then it says I admit I am in the wrong. If I go to Jerusalem and they crucify me, I follow in the footsteps of my master, and have hope that I will rise with him again at the end of days; and I remind you those days are not far off now.''

Agabus responded for all of us: ``The Lord's work will be done, whatever Paul or any of us should wish.''

In Jerusalem, we met Peter and James.

This whole trip was starting to feel like a journey backwards in time. I was half-expecting that our next stop would be to hear Jesus himself preaching, but that of course was impossible, at least in the flesh.

Given the purpose of this trip - to bring aid to the brothers in famine - our reception was surprising lukewarm. But given the aura of despair that had settled in the past couple of weeks, it was unsurprising at best.

``You should not have come,'' said James. ``You are not anybody's favourite person right now. And the fanfare which has announced your coming - everybody has been talking about the aid collection you have been raising across Greece and Asia - means that those who would plot against you have advance notice and have all the advantages. Even the brothers ...''

``The brothers are plotting against me?'' Paul asked, shocked.

``No of course not. What they say, they say openly. But it is not positive about you, brother.''

``What do they say?''

``The usual. That in bending to accomodate the legitimate issues raised in spreading the word you have gone apostate. That you wish to be the leader of our Way, and to do that you wish to collect more followers, more money ... as if the Spirit could be bought with mere money.''

``But you know those rumours are untrue! I follow the Law scrupulously in my own regard, except for the requirement to live apart from Gentiles ... an impossibility if you are on a mission to the Gentiles. And even so, I make it a rule to never actually live with Gentiles who have not converted to the Way.''

``What we know and what they say are neither here nor there. And in any case, your bigger issue is with those in the Temple who are not brothers but who plot against you. They are waiting for their moment to strike. You can be sure that they know you are here already and are probably laying their trap for you. You should not have come. I don't think you'll be able to leave alive.''

``Maybe not. That is for the Spirit to decide. Certainly all the prophets seem in accord for once that if I ever leave this city, it will not be as a free man. But this is where Jesus died for us. The least I can do is to follow his example to the end. How do you manage?''

``We, brother, live entirely within the Law. The early days when certain men prosecuted us because we preached Christ risen have passed. Some believe and join us; others do not and avoid us. But all of us continue in the Law and the traditions of the fathers. We are, as it were, considered a sect akin to the Pharisees and the Sadducees. And the fervour in these days is with the Romans. And many predict the destruction of Jerusalem. Is that a sign of the end times? How can we tell? Jesus was so vague ... just that the end times were assuredly coming.''

Paul sighed and thought.

``What then to do? I have come for two reasons. Yes, I could have put the collection in other hands, but the Spirit guided me not to. And I had sworn a Nazirite vow that I would not rest until I had personally ensured the money was in your hands. And so now I need to be in Jerusalem for the seven days of purification and discharge.''

Peter and James looked at each other. ``You're going to be here for seven days?'' said Peter. ``And do they know that?''

Paul looked at them askance. ``I'm not in the habit of keeping secrets,'' he responded. ``And even if I were, a Nazirite vow is not the easiest thing to try and keep secret. Of course I swore it publicly, and of course everybody knows. I'm surprised you didn't know.''

``Well, that answers one question at least,'' injected James. ``We have four brothers who went into debt and swore that they would live as Nazirites until they could find a way to repay their creditors.''

``Some of your brothers went into debt and the others did not help?'' said Paul. ``What happened to holding all in common?''

``You have been all around Asia collecting for us and yet you do not understand the gravity of the situation here? There is very little to be had. To say that some of our brothers are in debt is really just to distinguish those who have borrowed from those who have struggled to get by without borrowing.

``But here is what I propose. From the money you have collected you could settle their debts and then the five of you could go every day to the Temple to praise God and perform the necessary sacrifices. And our brothers would see you being holy, and being in the presence of those of our brothers that they know to be holy, and would know that the rumours that you have apostasized are simply untrue.''

Paul thought about this for a moment, pondering.

``And what you are not saying is that while I am here walking the streets of the city I will have a bodyguard of four men of impeachable character, well known to the authorities. And that snatching me away to the Sanhedrin under cover of darkness would be just that much harder.''

``It might be a side benefit,'' agreed Peter. ``But not what we will, but what the Father wills.''

``Indeed,'' agreed Paul, resigned.

I didn't go up to the Temple after the first day.

Obviously I'd wanted to see it, but to say that they make you feel like an outsider is an understatement. I don't imagine I'd feel any more out of place if I went to the Senate building in Rome - which is always known for its exclusivity. On top of which, I didn't feel comfortable drawing attention to the fact that Paul was surrounded by non-Jews. Timothy went with him, of course, but Timothy had not only joined the Way as a Gentile, but had gone `the whole way' and actually converted to Judaism, including having been circumsized.

That didn't mean to say that we weren't to be seen together around the city, and obviously our enemies whispered this and that against us: they said, rightly, that we were not circumsized and, wrongly, that our presence defiled others. Those `others' kept themselves pure by not living with us, bathing with us or eating with us. Being here was a shock given the frequent refrain of Paul's ``now there is neither Jew nor Gentile, slave or free man but we are all brothers in Christ who died and rose for us.''

On the seventh and final day of the purification, Paul had gone to attend the final purification ritual in the Temple, and I was at Peter's house when a messenger came running from the Temple to say that there had been a commotion and he thought Paul had been arrested by the Temple Guards.

``You think he's been arrested?'' Peter demanded. ``Why are you not sure?''

``The purification ritual had just been finished, and the five of them were walking out into the court of the Gentiles, and Trophimus was there waiting for them, and at that moment, some men dragged hold of Paul and Trophimus and started shouting `Blasphemy' and that Paul was defiling the Temple by bringing in foreigners and calling for the guards.

``And you know how it goes when a riot starts. If you don't get out quickly, then you often don't get out.''

Trophimus was one of our party; he was from Ephesus and had joined us at Miletus as the man responsible for bringing the Ephesus collection to Jerusalem and meeting with the brothers here. I hadn't seen him for a few days, but he wasn't one of the `inner circle', as it were. Our party had over twenty men in it and obviously not all of us were together all the time. But it was understandable that he had wanted to see Paul in his purified state. But would he really go into the parts of the Temple where Gentiles were forbidden?

``Without doubt, he stayed outside in the Court of the Gentiles where he was permitted,'' the messenger confirmed when Peter asked the question. ``But the facts are not that important in a case like this. He will testify, and I will testify that he was outside. But you can be sure that they will have a line of unimpeachable witnesses who say that he went inside. And who can know the Truth?''

``And was there a riot?'' interposed Peter's wife, concerned that the city might be overturned again by the rash of hot-blooded zealots on the loose for some kind of religious misdemeanour.

``Again, I do not know,'' the messenger demurred. ``My main concern was to escape and bring you the news before the riot started. But you know how these thing unfold: a man shouts `blasphemy', others tear their clothes and then all the old injustices and hatreds come out and before you know it the young hotheads are throwing stones and punching people at random. Then the guards come up to restore order and it becomes serious. And then if somebody decides to call the Romans out of the barracks ...''

``Right,'' said Peter. ``But we need more information. If there is a riot, the easiest thing would be for them to have Paul die `accidentally' in it. Otherwise there will probably have to be an arrest and a trial. I suppose they might want that, but probably they just want to get rid of him.

``Let's split up and find out what's happened. But Luke, you need to stay here. The last thing we want is them dragging you into it saying that you were defiling the Temple as well. That will be much harder for them if you're here.''

So I sat and waited. It was torture.
