6. Paul at Work

AD 50 Philippi

17 years after the crucifixion.

Paul was a hard worker.

Extracting true purple dye is a tricky business involving shellfish. It is not only tricky, it is slow, expensive and requires an enormous amount of shells to extract a small amount of dye. At Tyre, which has been the centre of the industry for as long as anyone can remember, the harbour and sea front are covered in the stinking, discarded shells of the used mollucs.

As a consequence of the expense and rarity of genuine purple dye, it was the almost exclusive province of kings, emperors and roman senators.

We did not make genuine purple dye, but a similar colour for those with exotic tastes - and merely a great deal of money.

The process we used depended on extracting a range of dyes from plants through crushing, then a chemical extraction involving urine. The resulting gloop was then passed through some filters and heated until a purple substance emerged.

Most of these steps would be considered unclean by a Jew.

Paul preached the risen Christ who forgave sins and made all who accepted him into their hearts as above those who merely obeyed the law. But for Paul himself, he insisted on following the Law scrupulously and could not engage in `unclean' practices. His one exception was that while Jews would not, in general, associate with or eat with Gentiles, Paul observed that God had shown him that there were not really Jews and Gentiles, but only children of God; brothers and sisters in Christ.

But for every step in our process that involves handling unclean materials, there are many supporting steps that do not. Paul could have worked selling the dye, or working the logistics that moved it from our factory to the four corners of the empire. Personally, I think he would have been good at either of those.

But he preferred what he called "honest work": something that you did with your hands and you could stand back and admire at the end of the day. So he cast amphorae. Not big amphorae, since our quantities were small. All morning long, he would sit at the potter's wheel, turning out elegant pots, then put them in the oven to fire them, then spend the afternoons decorating them and firing them again. Although the pot would be discarded once used, he felt that the vessel was the container that held the precious product and should be just as precious.

And while he worked he preached.

I did not work alongside Paul in the main. My duties as a doctor tended to take me all over the factory and estates, and often to our neighbours who were in need of medical assistance. But whenever the chance presented itself - if somebody in the factory needed help, or I just had a little time to myself - I would go down to where Paul worked, just to watch him and to listen to him.

``This pot,'' he was saying one time when I visited, ``can be used as a metaphor for the body. That which is truly valuable is what is on the inside - the soul. But the body, for as long as it lasts, for as long as it is needed to hold the soul, is valuable too.

``It must be taken care of, and should be as elegant and beautiful as possible. It should not be abused, or taken advantage of, just because it is not the soul, any more than the pot should be chipped just because it is not the dye.

``What then, if your soul has been saved through your knowledge of Christ? Shall you then abuse your body because your soul is safe? Surely not! Imagine if you were to chip away at this vessel with abandon. Could it not spring a leak? What then? The dye would escape and there would be nothing left.

``And so it is with the body and soul. What good if your soul is `saved', if it is allowed to leech away through the depravity of your body?

``It is not enough to say to yourself, `I do good when I can', but rather you need to live a wholesome life at all times.

``Listen to these words of Jesus which he spoke in the hills of Galilee:

``Love your enemies, do good to those who hate you, bless those who curse you, and pray for those who treat you badly.

``As for the man who hits you on one cheek, offer him the other one as well!

``And if a man is taking away your coat, do not stop him from taking your shirt as well. Give to everyone who asks you, and when a man has taken what belongs to you, don't demand it back.

``Treat men exactly as you would like them to treat you.

``If you love only those who love you, what credit is that to you? Even sinners love those who love them! And if you do good only to those who do good to you, what credit is that to you? Even sinners do that, and if you lend only to those from whom you hope to get your money back, what credit is that to you? Even sinners lend to sinners and expect to get their money back. No, you are to love your enemies and do good and lend without hope of return. Your reward will be wonderful and you will be sons of the most high. For he is kind to the ungrateful and the wicked!

``You must be merciful, as your father in Heaven is merciful.

``Don't judge other people and you will not be judged yourselves. Don't condemn and you will not be condemned. Make allowances for others and people will make allowances for you. Give and men will give to you - yes, good measure, pressed down, shaken together and running over will they pour into your lap. For whatever measure you use with other people, they will use in their dealings with you.''

``Paul,'' I said when he reached the end of this recitation, ``Lydia would like to see you when you have a convenient moment.''

``Do you know what it is that she wants?'' he asked.

``She wants to talk about the shipment of dye to the fullers' at Brindisi,'' I replied.

``And why are you the messenger,'' he asked.

``I'd hoped that we might be able to talk a little as we walked there.''

Paul tidied up his work fastidiously and made sure everything was ready for his return and that nothing would spoil in his absence. He looked at me watching him.

``Good measure, pressed down and shaken together,'' he said. ``Not just words, Luke, not just words but a philosophy for life.''

``Was Jesus a philosopher?'' I asked, only half seriously.

``What is a philosopher?'' Paul countered. ``I never met the man myself, but many of the things he said would have graced the lips of a Socrates, an Aristotle or a Seneca, don't you think? ``Just simpler and easier to understand.''

``Let us go to see the Lady,'' he said and started walking. ``What were you hoping to talk about?''

``I feel a burning need to do more,'' I confessed. ``Ever since my baptism, I feel a tearing hurry to be about God's work. This life - the only life I've ever really known - seems bland and - colourless, I suppose. Does that make sense?''

``I, too, feel a burning need to be about God's work,'' he replied. ``But is that not what I am doing here? I am bringing the word to the people of Philippi.''

``I don't know,'' I said. ``It seems so slow, so, piecemeal. I realize you're preaching as you work, but few people come by and hear it. And the preaching on the sabbath reaches so very few and they largely seem unwilling to listen to you.''

``I know; it is much the same everywhere I go. Small groups grow up - as with you here - around the core of the preaching and a few individuals - as you yourself - are infused with the Holy Spirit and can keep up the ministry when I am gone, but the majority of the Jews find the message that the Christ - for whom they have waited oh, these many years - has come to be unappealing. And, truly, I do understand, for once I was the same.''

True enough; his ardent espousal of the cause frequently caused me to forget his early days as an equally ardent opponent of the Way, but when he made comments like this, the whole story of his persecution of the Christians and his conversion in Damascus came flooding back. Of course he would relate to the Jews who opposed him: his former self had been one of them.

``So will you move on?''

``When the time comes, the Spirit will move me on,'' he replied, cryptically. ``We cannot know the times or the seasons, but God knows, and he will direct our paths.''

``Can I come with you?''

``Only time will tell, Luke. It is not for me to say, not least because you belong to another. Silas, along with Timothy and the others will come with me, so it is probably better that your place is here as one of the leaders of this community.''

We walked around the corner and up a short flight of steps into the main house building. We continued in silence around the colonnade to the atrium where Lydia would be waiting. As we parted, I made a final comment.

``I sense there is a better way.''

Paul looked at me, intently and inquisitively.

``A better way of spreading God's word than to go to people, meet them, share the gospel and demonstrate through your own life's actions the power of the word?''

Put like that, it was hard to come back to the point that I had been trying to make. But I was just about up to the task. I may only be a doctor, not an orator, but I like to think I have always had a way with words.

``Not necessarily better in that sense, but ... more efficient. A way of reaching more people in less time. And time, I feel, is of the essence.''

``I feel that too,'' he concurred. ``If you think of anything, let me know. But in the meantime, I must meet with the mistress of the house.''
