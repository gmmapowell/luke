10. Deliverance

AD 51 Philippi

14 years after the crucifixion

Paul's faith was unquenchable.

He kept at it, preaching the word of God in the prison all day long and throughout the first two watches of the night. We joined in with singing the Psalms when we knew them, especially the Psalm of the Shepherd, which seemed appropriate for this occasion, with its reference to walking within the very shadow of death.

Around midnight, when Paul was once more giving thanks for the deliverance he knew - or at least sincerely seemed to believe - was coming that night, the ground started to shake. At first gently, then with increasing force, a violent earthquake hit Philippi.

But unlike most such earthquakes, very little damage was done to most of the buildings: most, but not the prison.

With one almighty bang, the gates swung loose from their hinges and a great shout went up from within as the prisoners chains fell from the walls. Amazingly, they were all free to leave!

Outside, we were trembling with fear at the onset of the earthquake. An earthquake strong enough to topple the prison doors would surely knock over statues, bring down buildings and generally cause the kind of destruction that could put those of us out in the town square at risk of life and limb.

But Paul remained calm.

``Do not leave,'' we could hear him instructing the other prisoners. ``Although God has sent the earthquake to release us, it is simply a sign that we are not to be held responsible for the crimes of which we are accused. Let us wait here and await the magistrates, who will surely release us.''

The centurion had been in the barracks when the earthquake began and, being woken up by the violent shaking rushed out into the square and across to the prison, two other guards in his wake. I'm not quite sure what he expected to find when he arrived - the guards were still on duty inside the prison, although I suppose they could have been incapacitated.

But he certainly didn't expect Paul to be sitting there calmly and smugly with an `I told you so' look on his face.

``How, how ... how is this possible?'' the jailer asked.

``It is no secret,'' said Paul, ``and it is this very thing which I have been preaching openly - never in secret! - and for which I find myself here in jail. God the creator of the world, and all that is in it, does not accept the rush to judgment which has been made here in which innocent men have been thrown in jail.

``We have been delivered by the power of God, the power that underlies all the `powers that be' in the world. Indeed, your very authority comes from the ultimate authority of God, and you cannot gainsay him.

``When you choose to act against the will of God, you will always find, as you do today, that God's judgment will overturn the lesser judgment of men and cause them to seem foolish. For the wisdom of this world is indeed foolishness in God's eyes.''

The centurion looked at him aghast. Did this man really have the kind of power he claimed? The power to cause earthquakes and destroy the prison of Philippi?

``No, no,'' corrected Paul. ``I am not claiming any powers for myself. I am not a Wizard or Mage or suchlike. I am merely doing God's will, and it is God's will that I should be prevented from preaching the coming Kingdom of God on earth. When you stand in the way of that, bad things will happen.

``But never fear, the earthquake has passed and will not return. None will lose their lives in Philippi tonight.''

``You must come away from here,'' the Centurion commanded. ``Come to my house and my wife will bathe your wounds. I would like to hear more of this God you preach.''

``I have been preaching him openly this last half-day,'' said Paul. ``Were you not listening?''

``No,'' said the centurion. ``Loud as the noise you were making was, I tried very hard not to listen. I thought you were just trying to rile me.''

``Well,'' said Paul, ``fortunately I never tire of telling people of the coming Kingdom of God. Let us repair to your house and see what can be done by way of a meal, and I will open the gospel for you.''

``What then stands in the way of my baptism?''

Paul and his party had all been fed, and Lydia and I had been allowed to join them. The rest of our group had returned to their houses singing praises to the Lord of Heaven. Paul had not ceased in preaching the risen Christ and his deliverance from all sins.

Day was now very nearly breaking, and the Centurion and his family had been converted. As the Centurion himself realized, only the formalities remained.

``Nothing,'' replied Paul. ``When I am free to leave, we can go to the river and you will be baptised.''

``You are free to leave,'' responded the Centurion. ``Apart from anything else, it is clear that no prison walls will hold you.''

``I am not going to run away!'' replied Paul haughtily. ``I was arraigned through due process and I will be discharged through due process. Nothing less that a complete exoneration by the city's duumvirs will be acceptable!''

``But they were not to hear your case until tomorrow,'' replied the Centurion. ``How can I keep you until then if you are not guility?''

``I suggest that you summon them at once to their tribunal,'' said Paul. ``They cannot be ignorant of all that has passed in the night, and I'm sure that their sleep was disturbed. If for no better reason that an unfounded fear that further disturbances might plague their city, they should want a rapid resolution to this situation.''

The Centurion saw the logic of that at once and send one of his servants to go to the magistrates and tell them what had happened and ask for their advice.

Within minutes, the servant returned.

``They say the prisoners are free to go.''

The Centurion was pleased and beamed at Paul. ``All settled,'' he said. ``You and your colleagues are free to go. I will come with you as far as the river.''

``Not so fast,'' contradicted Paul. ``My arrest was open and public, in front of many witnesses of unimpeachable standing. My release needs to be just as formal. I insist on a hearing, in the public square, and for all the charges to be officially dropped.''

``You can't just tell the city magistrates what to do,'' complained the Centurion. ``They are as likely to throw you back in prison for impudence!''

``They are welcome to do so,'' countered Paul. ``They tried it once and it did them no good at all. You yourself observed that the prison itself will not hold us. No. The situation is very clear. The authority which the duumvirs have is entrusted to them from above, and they have been clearly told that this miscarriage of justice will not be tolerated. They will formally acquit me.''

The square was crowded.

Word had spread through the city of Paul's stand on his integrity; and not a single person had not felt the earthquake during the night. All who could manage to escape their daily activity had gathered in the square to see how the magistrates would deal with this unprecedented situation.

They had first gone to examine the damaged prison and assess how much effort it would take to repair. Then they had walked to their tribunal and summoned the Centurion and, when he appeared, told him to bring the prisoners.

Paul and Silas arrived in front of them, heads held high.

``You are brought here on a charge of disturbing the peace,'' the senior duumvir intoned. ``And yet, when we lock you up in prison, you choose to destroy it.''

``I am not careful in speaking to you,'' interrupted Paul. ``For I fear neither man nor spirit. It was not I who caused the earthquake, but God himself. And against him, your so-called justice cannot stand.''

The magistrates eyed him wearily. The people had seen a miracle, and this convinced them that these men were in the right. But the magistrates knew that he was a troublemaker, and that the last thing the Romans wanted was men in authority who could not manage to keep the peace. It was beyond them to do anything in the current situation except figure out a compromise and moved these men on to some other town where they could cause their trouble and heartache to another magistrate.

``Even so,'' he declaimed slowly, ``the fact remains that you have disturbed the peace, and in these times that can carry quite severe penalties. It has been made clear to us that however noble your intentions may be, and it certainly seems to us that you are only acting from the best of motives and with a genuine desire and regard for the well-being of our citizens, your presence here is a lodestone for trouble.

``We observe that none of your party are citizens of Philippi - although you have clearly gained some followers in your time here who are noble citizens - and thus we feel the most appropriate course of action is to release you and ask you to leave our fair city. We are told that you are somewhat itinerant, so in other words, maybe this is just your god nudging you to move on, eh?''

``If God wills that I should move on,'' replied Paul stiffly, ``then move on I shall. By midnight I will be gone from among your dwellings, but the grace of God will remain in your presence by the will of those whose hearts are open.''

``The prisoners are released, under penalty of being exiled from the city. If they are found within the city walls after daybreak tomorrow, they will be considered outlaw.''

The baptism at the riverside felt sombre.

The Centurion and his family went into the river and emerged rejuvenated and filled with the Holy Spirit. Like so many of the rest of us, he asked Paul if he could share in his travels and leave with him, but Paul denied him - as he had me - on the grounds that the branch of the Way in Philippi was still young and needed all the nurturing and assistance that was to be had.

Lydia and I waited on the riverbank, knowing that we would probably never see Paul again. In spite of the gifts that he had brought - and was leaving with - us, the world would seem less bright without his personal presence.

The sun was moving down in the sky by the time all the rituals were over and Paul came back to us. He looked at us as if unclear as to why we were waiting there, but apart from anything else, a baptism is always a beautiful and moving experience.

``I do need to keep moving,'' he said. ``This is but one small corner of Europe, and I have much to do. Thessalonica is just down the road, then Athens, Piraeus and the stews of Corinth! Then moving on up to Delphi and across to Italy: Syracuse, Naples and Rome!''

``Beware of Rome,'' said Lydia with a shudder. ``I sense that no good will come to you there.''

``I have heard of other members of the way visiting Rome already,'' said Paul. ``It is said that Claudius is relatively open to Jews and was friends with that snake Herod. But in any case, it will be a few years before I make it there; and even that is only a stepping stone on the way to Spain and the Gauls.''

``And Britain?'' I joked. None of us really had much of an idea what was there, but it had been a half-dozen years before that Claudius - with great fanfare - had announced his conquest of the island. A conquest that, we heard, had subsequently become bogged down in minor scuffles against painted tribesmen. Paul's mixture of Hebrew myths and speculative philosophy would probably not go down well there.

``Why not?'' retorted Paul. ``The end days are coming! Should the painted Britons not also be allowed to partake in the general salvation at the end days?''

``Prepare to close the gates.''

A few hours later, as dusk was settling, we were by the South Gate again. Paul, Silas, Timothy and the rest had just left, carrying their few possessions with them. We had remained by the gate, watching until they were moving out of sight over the hill.

And now the guards were sealing the city as they did every night; locking out the brigands and marauders who were not enough to literally threaten the town, but who could do mischief if allowed inside overnight.

The gates closed and banged, and darkness fell.

We walked back home.
