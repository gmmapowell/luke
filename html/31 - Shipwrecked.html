30. Shipwrecked

AD58 The Mediterranean

25 years after the crucifixion

The storm was unremitting.

Everything had gone smoothly until we had reached Crete, when the storm had come rushing out of the north-east, blowing over the island and rushing us out to sea.

Festus had been as efficient in his dispositions as he had been in his judgment. The day after the judgment, a centurion was detailed to take the prisoner, along with two slaves - myself and Timothy were chosen - to Rome. By that evening, the four of us, together with a bodyguard of two decuries - a further sixteen soldiers and four slaves - were on board a ship running from Caesarea up the coast which was sailing to Myra in Lycia.

But from the moment we had left Sidon, our first stop, everything had started to go against us. The wind was blowing in our faces for much of the time, forcing us to go further north than we had wished and follow the coast of Asia, before attempting to cross over and use the coastline of Cyprus to avoid the direct wind and garner the spilloff to drive us westwards.

Eventually we had made it to Myra, and we debated waiting until the spring trading season opened up and the winds would be more predictably westward. But Julius, the centurion, managed to find a captain from Alexandria who was planning on making a final corn run to Rome and agreed to take us. At least, we had joked, we would not be short of food on this trip.

We sailed as far as Cnidus without incident, although slowly and the wind continued to be against us. Here we debated once again waiting for spring, but the captain said that we should be able to run south easily enough and make Crete. From there, we could either winter in Phoenix easily enough or continue on our journey depending on the winds.

The leg southwards had gone smoothly enough and we made it to Crete without incident, but when we turned westwards, we again struggled to beat against the headwinds. We reached a small port called Fair Havens, which the captain judged unsuitable to wait out until the spring brought new weather conditions.

Paul had argued vigorously that he had foreseen doom and shipwreck should we continue past Fair Havens, but since another day would see us safe in the snug harbour at Phoenix, the captain - and the centurion - were disinclined to accept his viewpoint: after all, he was just a prisoner.

But it was during that day that the storm had suddenly blown up; coming from the land as it had, we had had no recourse to attempt to make any harbour but were swept uncontrollably southwards.

The captain had immediately ordered the sails struck, but the storm had been so fierce that much of the tackle had been damaged or destroyed and was swinging around the deck in wild gyrations and had to be cut loose and sent overboard.

As the waves had grown, they threatened to swamp the heavily-laden vessel and sink us. The experienced crew knew the only remedy: lighten the ship by any expedient. First the ballast, then the valuable cargo went overboard, and soon the only weight on the ship was the passengers and essential supplies - along with the water we tried desperately to bail out day and night.

We had now been in this state for over a week.

With the rain lashing down and the wind blowing, Paul walked to the main mast, now cracked in two places. Holding onto it with all his strength, he called out in that voice which those who had heard his preaching knew so well: not the voice of Paul the man, but the voice of Paul the divinely inspired messenger of God.

``Listen, well, oh valiant sailors. I have word from God. The God who made the heavens and the earth, the God who made the seas. Listen, for he has sent his word to me and it is this I tell you now.

``Surely this ship will be lost. I have seen it shipwrecked on an island. But in my vision, one thing above all was clear: the cargo has been lost, the ship will yet die, but not one of us will be harmed or lose life in the tragedy!

``Stand firm and trust in the power of the Lord! The God who made all things, who raised his Christ from the grave has promised this much! We will all stand on dry land again after the destruction of this ship!

``Listen to the words of God: God requires that I should testify of him before Caesar. God requires that I should be in Rome, and thus, as a measure of his power, all of our lives are saved. God is just and merciful, and to all here mercy is shown.''

Understandably, this speech amazed all those on board. Nobody could quite believe either the certainty with which he spoke or the arrogance to assume that their lives were subordinate to a god's desire to save a mere prisoner.

Nevertheless, as the next few days passed, and the ship remained afloat in spite of having become almost a complete wreck, the words, and the conviction with which they were spoken - together with Paul's continuing positive attitude - the crew started to believe that maybe some divine agency had chosen them to live.

When the storm finally released us, we drifted for a number of days. The crew attempted to effect repairs, but the mast was too badly damaged to carry the driving force of the wind needed to move the ship forward. But Paul remained adamant that we would be saved.

Finally, overnight one of the sailors on `lookout' shouted that he could smell land. It's a funny thing, but when you are on land, you can smell the salt tang of the sea; but when you have been at sea for a few weeks, it is land that has the noticeable smell.

The captain immediately called out for soundings, and found the bottom to be shoaling at twenty fathoms; a turn later, it was fifteen. He promptly ordered that all the remaining anchors be thrown out and grasp the ship where it was.

At daybreak, the land appeared in the rising light. None of us aboard recognized it, but we had no hope of going anywhere else. There was a current sucking us in, so we released the anchors and drifted inwards as slowly as we could manage.

When we were nearly ashore, the ship ran aground on a shoal. After weeks of torment at sea, the ship had lost the integrity needed to have its bows held fast in the sandbar while the current and wind battered the stern: it started to break apart. The crew began to desert the ship, but the soldiers planned to kill we three prisoners. Julius, however, had by this time believed what Paul had said, and felt that by killing any of us - especially Paul - that he would be endangering everybody's lives.

Instead, he organized the remaining crew, soldiers and prisoners into teams to ensure that as many as possible reached the land. All Roman soldiers, since the time of Gaius Marius, had been expected to know how to swim, and they were put in charge of the teams. Using broken parts of the ship, they created floats for those who could not swim, and ensured that in each team an appropriate balance of swimmers and non-swimmers could assist each other for the half-mile trip to the island.

Nobody died. Whether this was because of God's intervention or the careful organization of the Roman centurion, I could not say. I believed in both implicitly.

When we came ashore, we were tired, wet and cold. More than that, we were exhausted from our two week ordeal at sea. Fortunately for us, we were not the first ship to have wrecked on that coastline and the locals, having seen our ship in distress since dawn, had prepared for us coming ashore: they had gathered wood for a fire, brought bread and meat and sent a runner to the governor, who owned the estate near where we had landed.

As Paul came up to the fire, to warm himself and to dry out his sodden clothes, a snake came hissing out at him - presumably it had been collected with the logs making up the fire - and sank its teeth into his hand. He immediately cried out, but then shook his hand, releasing it and it slithered away.

``Neither this snake nor I will see any hurt this day,'' he declared. ``God, who is a God of Love and not hatred, will not cause harm to any of us gathered hear today.'' And so saying, he took some of the bread that the locals had brought and, in his characteristic fashion, looked up, gave thanks for our deliverance, and broke the bread.

By now, not just the crew but the locals as well believed that something miraculous was happening among them. This man was not just brave, he was clearly inspired by some divine power. Some even said that he was a god himself.

The following day the governor himself sent for us. He had been told of our arrival and wanted to find out our business. When he found that the party was not just civilians but contained a military delegation, he promptly arranged to house us himself, attached to his own regiment.

We walked over to his residence, a journey of over ten miles, and no mean feat for a group of men who had been exhausted and shipwrecked. But Paul preached and declaimed the whole way, and not one of the men failed to make the journey.

When we arrived, we were greeted by the governor in his purple-bordered toga. He showed us into his state dining room and made dispositions for the crew and guards.

``So you are Paul, citizen of Tarsus?'' he opened. ``I have heard a lot about you, one way and another. I was in Athens when you preached there, if you remember. It caused quite a stir.''

``You were the governor of Greece?'' enquired Paul. ``I don't remember you.''

``No, I was not the governor,'' he replied. ``It is only now I have been appointed a governor, and not of a province, either, but only of this miserable rock, strategically important as it may be.

``No, I was in Athens studying rhetoric from the great teachers. And we had heard that there was a man in the city claiming to have seen the dead walking and to be announcing two new gods. And then you were called for inquisition before the city council on the Areopagus. Do you remember?''

``That I remember very well,'' admitted Paul. ``But still, I do not remember you. I take it you were there.''

``As I say, I was studying rhetoric in Athens. My teachers felt that it would be a good opportunity to learn by evaluating your performance from a rhetorical standpoint. I have to say, you were a disappointment.''

The shock on Paul's face was self-evident. He was not used to this reaction. Some people understood, were converted, repented of their sins and joined the brotherhood. Others were outraged, felt threatened, lashed out and tried to stone him, beat him, arrest him or kill him.

But `a disappointment'?

``You clearly had not studied even the basic rhetorical forms,'' the governor continued. ``You had passion and drive, it is true. I will even accept that you believed what you so passionately declared. But there was no skill there. To a Greek or Roman, you lacked the polish that makes for a convincing argument.''

Paul remained taken aback. It was a few moments before he regrouped.

``So you judge a man not on whether he speaks the truth but on how many rhetorical flourishes he can manage?'' Paul exclaimed. ``That is madness! But no wonder I failed in Athens. Corinth with its stews was much more receptive.''

``It is not a matter of truth or not-truth,'' replied the governor. ``We all know that there are many truths and many philosophies. Clearly, then, how you express them is as important as any alleged truth you may possess.''

``But God himself has intervened in the world. He has overcome death itself and proclaimed the start of the end times through the bodily resurrection of Jesus Christ - ''

The governor held up his hand. Not a rhetorical flourish as such, but a gesture Paul could ignore only at his peril.

``If you have proof, proof you can show me, then that is a separate matter. But in the meantime, do not burden me with your poor rhetoric.''

Proof? What is proof?

The conversation continued with Julius giving a detailed account of our trip. He explained his reasoning for sailing from Crete, and the governor approved his logic. He agreed to write a note to his commander exonerating him for the loss of the ship and, further, agreed to compensate the ship's captain from his stategic fund.

We were discussing the nature of storms in the Mediterranean when a servant came in and whispered to the governor.

``I'm sorry,'' he said, ``but my father is sick and may be dying. I must go and see him.''

``I am a doctor,'' I said, ``may I come with you?''

He generously allowed Paul to come along as well. If nothing else, he said, it might be pleasant to pass his last few hours listening to our adventures.

We walked into the sick chamber and the smell was that of death: I knew it well. I went up to the patient and examined him. But Paul cried out:

``The mercy of God through Jesus in the resurrection is extended to this man! It has been said that none involved in the shipwreck shall die, and I say that this man is involved! He will not die.''

And immediately Paul knelt by the beside, placed his hands on the man's head and prayed.

When he released him, the governor's father sat up, stood up and came and joined us for dinner.

By the time we left three months later, we left a significant community of believers behind us.
