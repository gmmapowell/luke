14. Journey to Caesarea

AD 56 Judaea

23 years after the crucifixion

I can't speak for anyone else - although I can certainly imagine how they felt - but I was in shock after this speech.

I had just been sold from the only mistress I had ever known into the hands of a man I loved dearly and would follow to the ends of the earth - only to find he was hurrying to meet his betrayal and death!

After Paul's address ended, I went down to the harbour and boarded our ship. We were due to set off at first light and try to make it directly to Cos. From there, Paul was hoping that he would be able to board another ship that went straight from there to Caesarea, but no matter what, he was going to keep going.

Paul remained in the town for a long while and parted with old friends one by one. Emotional as the moment was for me, I could only imagine what it was like for him.

Around me was a hive of activity. Apart from our own baggage - and the collection from Ephesus - that was being brought on board, the shipmaster was still loading his main cargo, and other passengers eager to make an early run from Asia to the far end of the Mediterranean were boarding along with their possessions. In the dark, lit only by guttering torches, the clamour of the stevedores seemed preternaturally loud. And that, set against what Paul had just revealed ...

Paul boarded almost literally as they were pulling up the gangplank and preparing to cast off. Even so, he complained that he had to hurry away from some of the brothers he still considered lifelong friends - even though by his own admission he would never see them again.

The morning was bright and we had a firm breeze blowing us offshore. The captain assured us that with any luck the wind would hold and we would make good time to Cos.

As long as there wasn't a storm.

The sentence that any good sailor - and certainly and good captain - utters almost reflexively the moment they have expressed any opinion about what the notoriously fickle sea may choose to do. None of us wanted to lose our lives this night.

Paul was leaning over the port rail when I finally plucked up the courage to ask him what he thought he was doing.

To put it bluntly, he looked at me like I might have gone mad.

``I am going to Jerusalem to fulfill a number of vows.'' He started to enumerate them.

``I have sworn a Nazirite vow, and little though that may mean to you, trust me, it is a very serious matter and I must go to the temple to discharge it.

``Secondly, I must deliver the monies that I have collected from across Asia and Greece to help the poor and needy brothers in Jerusalem.

``Thirdly, Silas has business in Jerusalem and he, too, must go to the Temple to make an offering.

``And finally, and quite unanswerably, I go because that is where the Holy Spirit sends me.

``Do you really think that anything I do is done of my own free will? You think you know me, Luke, because of the time we spent together in Philippi. But that is but a small slice of my life and experience. It may feel threatening to you to go to Jerusalem knowing that they will try to kill me. But remember, that everywhere I have already been they have tried to kill me. What makes Jerusalem different? Only that it is where the head of the snake is. Cut that off, and we are free!''

``And do you think you can defang the snake in Jerusalem?'' I asked. Nothing I had heard so far suggested he had a firm belief in his own survival, let alone such a stunning victory.

``No, I do not,'' he responded. ``I am not so arrogant as to believe that I can accomplish what Jesus could not. But I know that everything that happens, happens at the direction of the Spirit.

``And the Spirit cannot want that it should die out from the world and that none should be saved. So if I need to go to Jerusalem and give my freedom - or my life - for the greater good, I will go and give whatever is demanded of me. And I will go always testifying to the Truth as it has been revealed to me. And perhaps I will escape the trap, or perhaps I will turn it on them, or perhaps just in failing I make a difference that saves one more soul.

``Remember, Luke, that there will be more rejoicing in Heaven over just one sinner who repents than a thousand righteous men who followed the Law and did not need to repent! My life for the redemption of a sinner? How could I say no?''

I looked at him and knew that he was sincere. I knew also that my fate was tied to his - and had been since that first fateful night in Troas. In reality, it had been since long before that; since we had first heard those whispers of a new doctrine coming out of Judea, bringing with it hope of the resurrection among men. If he was going to Jerusalem, I was going to Jerusalem. And if he was going to die there, there was an above-evens chance that I would die there, too.

There were no ships sailing directly from Cos to the East, so we had sailed on to Rhodes as the most likely place to find such a ship.

We didn't, but the harbourmaster directed us to a ship bound for Patara, where he said that the Phoenecians would have a number of ships heading for Tyre, and from there it would be easy to find a coastal lugger going to Caesarea or we could avoid the vagaries of the sea and just travel overland.

We'd followed his advice and were now south of Cyprus heading for the ship's home port of Tyre. Day by day we continued in our practices, rituals and traditions and broke bread together and prayed for understanding and repentance. Paul had given over preaching for the course of this journey for reasons that he kept to himself. It certainly wasn't for fear, but it was almost as if he were retreating inside himself.

After the morning prayers, Silas dared to ask one of the questions we were all wondering. ``Why the great rush?'' he asked. ``Why is it so important to reach Jerusalem by Pentecost?''

Paul looked at him intently; then sighed deeply.

``I do not know,'' he replied. ``I only know that I am guided by the Spirit; I am driven by the Spirit; I am constrained by the Spirit. And the Spirit impels me forward and shows me that something will be happening at Pentecost and I need to be there.

``And if the Spirit knows that I need to be there, it will make it come true. And if I am not there, that too will have its reasons. But it is not given to me to know or understand the reasons and so I cannot pass that on to you. But I sense that I must do all in my power to be in Jerusalem by Pentecost.''

And that was all there really was to say on the subject. We were bound to the will of the Spirit.

We had an easy journey to Tyre and still had three weeks left before Pentecost. One of the brothers in Tyre had a ship doing the coastal run through Caesarea and down to Alexandria and offered us passage leaving in a week. Since the journey would be about a week and it was only a couple of days from there to Jerusalem we agreed eagerly, especially since it gave us a week to spend with a community that knew Paul well and were keen to catch up with him after he had been away for many years.

On the Sabbath we had our weekly prayer meeting and offered thanks to God for our deliverance from sin through the intercession of Christ Jesus. When Paul had spoken at length, others rose to speak and one after another stood and prophesied that if Paul were to step within the Temple he would never taste freedom again.

Eventually Paul snapped. Always irritable and ready to rush to judgment, this was not what he wanted to hear right now.

``What use is it to prophesy the same thing over and over?'' he demanded. ``What do you want me to do? If it be the end of my life; if I be tortured; if I be arrested and held in a dungeon; what is that to me? They can destroy this body of my earthly habitation but I will not let them corrupt my soul by running away from the Truth that has been revealed to me by the Holy Spirit.

``I confess that you have heard the words of the Spirit, and I too know that no bodily good will come from this trip, even though it be a trip of mercy for those who would persecute me. But tell me, what can I do? I can no more run away from the demands of the Spirit than you can contain these prophecies. Enough! I am content that in all things I have been honest and high-minded with you; that I have told you all the truth as it was given to me; and that when I am no more, you will hold to the truth in your community.

``And if I go to Jerusalem and do not return, then we will see each other in the day of the glory of the Lord when he returns for us.''

He paused and looked around the room; then as soon as the temper had flared up, it settled down and he calmed himself and sat down again.

``I'm sorry,'' he said. ``Please continue, but know that no matter what anybody says or does, I am bound for Jerusalem. I have no choice, come what may.''

Two days later we left Tyre and sailed via Ptolemais to Caesarea where we left the ship. As we walked to the harborside in Tyre, all the brothers had come with us, wanting their last glimpse of Paul. As at Philippi, they knew they were never going to see him again.

Caesarea was a centre of the Way unlike any I had seen before.

Our small community in Philippi was little more than an extension of Lydia's household. In Troas, the meetings I had attended had had forty or fifty men present, but many of those were not believers but others that were curious or that Paul was trying to convert. In Ephesus, there were maybe twenty or thirty brothers who had made the pilgrimage to Miletus to see Paul when we had passed through.

Here in Caesarea, you could be forgiven for thinking that almost the whole city belonged to the Way. It wasn't true of course, but the fact that most of the `real' residents (as opposed to Roman officials or Greek merchants) were Jews meant that there was a much wider pool of potential converts to draw from. And a lot of drawing had gone on.

In many of the original `Jesus stories', the primary characters were fishermen. I'd not been to Galilee - this was the first time I'd visited the province of Judea, in fact - but it made sense that in preaching to a group of rustics who mainly made their living farming or fishing and selling to the big Roman towns like Sepphoris, he would have used metaphors and stories grounded in that lifestyle.

And it made sense that that terminology would have been preserved and passed on and amplified even after the communities spread to the big cities - Jerusalem and Caesarea being the biggest in Judea proper.

The community here was led by a man called Philip, a man with a certain reputation in the community of the Way - enough that, much like Peter and James - I had heard of him. He was not one of the original disciples who had followed Jesus from his earliest days, but had been a convert early on when John was still baptizing by the Jordan. He had been a follower of John and then, when John was arrested, had gone to see Jesus preach and been impressed with the stylistic differences. John had been a fire-breathing prophet who could almost have walked out of the pages of the Holy Writings; Jesus was a very calm, understated man but one with an undeniable authority. It was like meeting the nobleman's son or one of the great Roman commanders. He didn't rant or rave or demand obedience, he merely told you how it was with complete authority and self-confidence. From there, it was all but impossible to argue with him. I'd heard - and I was to hear much more over the coming years - that in his presence it was all but impossible to hold to former, `sinful' beliefs. It wasn't that he told you to repent; to be in his presence was to repent. Anything else just felt like madness.
