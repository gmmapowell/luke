17. Claudias Lysias

AD56 Jerusalem

23 years after the crucifixion

The messenger had not exaggerated the danger.

I was finally able to see Colonel Claudias Lysias, in charge of the Roman Regiments in Jerusalem, two days after Paul's arrest and obtain the full story of what had passed. It seems that Colonel Lysias had saved Paul's life not once but twice in the intervening time, and when I met with him was preparing to save it for a third time.

When he had arrived on the scene, he explained to me, Paul was on the verge of being ripped bodily limb from limb.

``I don't understand this city, I don't mind admitting,'' he told me once I had won his confidence and reassured him that I was not spying either for the Governor or the Jewish leaders but was a concerned friend of Paul's who honestly believed that he was innocent of any crimes. ``Give me some good Germans or Thracians any day. At least up there on the border you know who the enemy are and what you need to do to achieve your goals. Bash a few heads and kill a few tribesmen and the rest run away with their tails between their legs.

``But here, I don't get it. They have so many different divisions and so many different perspectives, it makes me head spin. And all about something so abstract, how can you even know what the truth is? Give me a good body of flesh and blood rather than this ridiculous religious speculation.

``Anyway, it seems they were having some argument about the nature of the God. A lot of the shouting was in Aramaic, and I just don't know that. My Greek ain't too bad, but basically I'm a Roman and I speak Latin. So simple concepts in Greek is one thing, but a religious discourse in Aramaic. I ain't a clue.

``But I know one thing: when twenty men are pummelling one, he probably needs my protection. So I ordered my men and we waded in - just shields, you understand, no swords drawn and pulled him out of there and off to the barracks.''

I have to day, where Paul's safety was concerned, I'd personally rather he had drawn his sword and killed a few of the blighters attacking him, but I saw his point. I've seen enough riots to know that drawing swords just leads to mindless death and more riots the next day ... or the day after. This was clearly a man who had reached his position through skill and experience and not just by political favour - so often the case in this decadent age.

``At first, I mistook him for one of those zealots. You know, once of those professional troublemakers who go around causing trouble. We had a guy here not so long ago from Egypt who'd raised almost a legion of rabble and tried to raise the countryside. We put that down of course, right sharpish, but the ringleader escaped. From all accounts, he looks like this Paul of yours. But when I interrogated him, it became clear that he wasn't one of these Alexandrian Jews who are all uppity since they helped out the Great Caesar a hundred years ago - Jupiter bless his memory! - but from Tarsus, which, as he was at pains to point out, while not Alexandria, is still a city of reputation.''

``I was going to have him scourged and let him go, but apparently he has connections somewhere up or down the Imperial chain through his family back in Tarsus, so I figured that we'd better do things by the book. And here, that means first and foremost putting him on trial before the local authorities. Since this seemed to be some kind of religious dispute, I decided to take him in front of the Sanhedrin.''

The Sanhedrin! A bigger snakepit of Paul's dedicated enemies could hardly be found. There was no chance that they would have acquited him - after all, they were basically the very people who had set him up in the first place. Why set him up if the didn't plan on following through and finding him guilty?

``They can be very touchy and with all their restrictions and everything is often very hard to arrange a trial. They won't come here and we can't go there and all that. So eventually we managed to find one of their leaders' houses that was sufficiently `profane' to allow us in with the prisoner while still being acceptable for their meeting.

``And again, they went off into Aramaic, and I'll be confounded if I'd could understand the process. No lofty oratory, just a lot of confused shouting, and a couple of times they just struck the prisoner when he said things they didn't like. In the end, he said something that threw them all into consternation, and half of them seemed to be ready to acquit him while the other half seemed ready to rip him limb from limb on the spot like the mob the day before.

``So I had no choice, did I? I had to intervene and pull him out of there. You can ask him yourself about it, but basically he said they were arguing about the afterlife. Seems dumb to me ... was he planning to get himself killed just to find out what happens after?''

``So can I see the prisoner?'' I asked.

``Sure, follow me,'' he replied.

Paul seemed brighter than I'd expected when I finally saw him.

But then, I had to realize that being reviled and beaten was hardly a new experience for this man. In the short time I'd been with him, I'd probably seen it three or four times. And now this.

``But at least the worst is over!'' he enthused when I greeted him. ``The prophecies have been fulfilled and I am still here to witness for the Lord!''

It was true that he was still alive but `here' being the Roman prison on the outskirts of Jerusalem, I'm not entirely sure it's where I'd aspire to be.

``The Lord Jesus appeared to me again last night,'' said Paul.

What? Jesus had returned to the city of his death and I'd missed it! No wonder Paul was so uplifted!

``He stood where you are standing and reassured me, that while I may be a captive here, I will not die here. But that I will be taken to Rome - and reach there alive!"

Taken to Rome? For what purpose? What was Paul talking about?

``Slow down,'' I reproved him. ``I'm not sure you're making any sense. Let's start at the beginning. What happened at the Temple.''

``We had made our final offering to discharge our vow,'' he began, frowning as he tried to recollect the events. We had walked outside, and I was in the court of the Gentiles when Trophimus - you know, the representative from the Ephesus brotherhood - came up to me and congratulated me. Of course, he's not circumsized, he doesn't follow the strict Law. He's allowed in the Court of the Gentiles, but not in the Temple. But then some fool came up yelling he'd been in the inner Courts of the Temple when I'd been discharging my vow. Ridiculous, of course!

``But it was enough for a crowd of men to come around and start grabbing and attacking me.''

``And yelling `blasphemy','' I added, recalling what the messenger had said at Peter's house.

``Indeed,'' conceded Paul. ``It was cunningly done. I don't know if they knew that Trophimus was going to be there - or if they somehow even managed to plant that thought in his mind. But they certainly knew enough to take advantage of the situation when it arose.

``The Romans intervened of course - I'm not really sure whether to be grateful about that or not - and carted me off here.''

``And then sent you to the Sanhedrin for trial.''

``Trial? A farce. Those buffoons think they can intimidate me, and into what? I really don't understand where they're coming from. I think they are in fact most scared of the thought that Jesus might have been what we preach him to me - literally the Son of Man, come to announce the end times and the beginning of the judgment.

``And so much of what they think and do is based on the essentially human aspects of their job. They love the status, the sense of feeling better than their flock. And they probably reasonably fear for the day when not only will they no longer be better than the others - for we shall all see each other as adopted sons of God - and not even better than the Gentiles! - but will themselves be judged; and how could they not be found wanting?''

``How did you manage to avoid execution?''

``I tried to convince them of the error of their ways, and tried once more to witness to them the truth of Jesus dead and raised. And when I brought up the subject of the resurrection, all the party of the Sadducees ripped open their blasphemy straps and yelled that there was no resurrection. And then it struck me!

``Much of the persecution we face is not because of the fear of apostasy, but simply because there is a large group of priests who deny that there will be a resurrection at the last day.

``And when we preach that not only is the hope of resurrection extended to all Jews, and beyond that all Gentiles, but that our faith is based on the evidence of our own senses that one has risen from the dead already, their entire world view is threatened. How can they continue to claim that there is no resurrection, when one has stood amongst us, resurrected in incorruptible flesh?''

``And for this they would kill you?'' I was incredulous.

``For this, and for so much more,'' he responded. ``They fear the truth and the promise of God. They fear the mercy that I preach. They fear the Gentiles and the possibility that their institutions would be overrun by newcomers ...''

``Another visitor for the prisoner,'' shouted the guard on duty and the messenger I'd seen at Peter's house a couple of days before came in.

``Hello, nephew,'' said Paul, with his familiar greeting to all those to young to truly be called `brothers' in the faith. ``What's wrong?''

``More bad news, I'm afraid,'' the boy replied. ``They won't stop, you know that, don't you?''

``Who? The Sanhedrin?'' asked Paul.

``Well, the younger, more radical ones,'' the boy said. ``I was in the market square this morning and a group of them came by. At that time, it was suspicious that they were there, so I followed them - discreetly, you can be sure - and tried to overhear what they were saying. It was difficult to make out, but the basic plot appears to be that they want to arrange for a second hearing and then grab you and kill you en route into the city. You need protection.''

``Well, there's only one place that's going to come from,'' I said. ``That's the Romans. Colonel Claudius Lysias seems to think you worth preserving. I've formed something of a relationship with him; I'll go and advise him to make sure he puts a decent bodyguard on you.''

Paul's `nephew' came with me to see the Colonel. First-hand reporting, even of the somewhat vague kind, is always worth having. We had to wait to see him, as he was meeting with someone else. Finally we were admitted to this office.

``Colonel,'' I began, ''at some point this morning, you should expect to receive a request from the Sanhedrin to examine the prisoner Paul again.''

``Do you just say this because the High Priest's representative has just left and you are guessing at his motives, or do you have independent information?'' he asked.

I pointed to the boy, who spoke up.

``I heard them discussing a plot to take Paul's life,'' he said. ``The plan is to demand Paul's summons to the Sanhedrin and then attack the understrength body of guards on the way up the hillside. In the fracas Paul dies. And then I think they intend to pass off the blame on us.''

``I smelt something fishy when that man was here. He wouldn't answer my questions directly, and avoided my gaze. On top of which, I'm not sure that this religious court has jurisdiction over this case - if there is a case.

``I may not know Jewish law, but I understand Roman law and practice. It's more than my job's worth to not inform the governor. And I think at this moment the right thing is to send the prisoner with my message.''

``Scribe! Come here!''

The scribe quickly came according to the summons and he started dictating his message to Governor Felix. ``Take down this message and make fair copies for the archives and to send to Rome.

``Claudius Lysias to the Governor Felix. Salutations! I am sending this message with a prisoner whom I arrested for his own safety at the Temple of the Jews in the midst of a riot when he was on the point of being murdered by the mob. Since then, I have had the opportunity to interrogate him and he does not appear to be a subversive or to have committed any crime recognized under Roman law. He claims citizenship of Tarsus in Asia and to be connected to eminent - Roman - citizens there. Wishing to find out what the accusation was that they were making against him, I had him brought down to their Sanhedrin. There I discovered he was being accused over questions of their laws, but they had no charge against him which deserved either death or imprisonment. Now I have received that there is a plot against his life. This all goes above my authority and thus I am sending him to you without delay. My recommendation is that you release him, but at the same time I have notified his accusers that if they wish to continue their prosecution of him they must come to Caesarea and make their charges against him in front of you."

While the scribe was taking down this dictation and slowly reducing it to perfect copy, he made all the other logistical arrangements with a precision and division of intellect which I found amazing. With individuals like this in charge of their military machine, it's not surprising that the Romans have conquered the world.

He summoned one of his centurions in charge of the infantry battalion and told him to prepare an escort for Paul.

``I want you to put 200 men on the job. I don't imagine for one minute that there are anything like that number of Jews in on this plot, so just that number will be a big deterrent. You shouldn't have to fight. But on top of that take another seventy skirmishers ... slingers or something like that in case they decide to try and just take out Paul without a pitched engagement.''

Then he summoned the centurion in charge of the cavalry, which Romans generally despised as a military arm but he recognized they had their uses:

``How many men have you available right now for a trip to Caesarea? Good. Take seventy of them and spread them out along the column, but put at least a dozen around the prisoner to shield him. Make him walk until you're out into the open country, then you can mount him up to make better time. Space the others out so as to be ready to charge down anyone trying to hit and run. I don't want to start a riot, but if it means that much to these criminals I'll give them a fair fight. Rules of engagement: don't start anything but be sure to appear menacing and the moment anything happens, I want you to respond with overwhelming force. I pre-idemnify you against any locals that may be trampled if the prisoner makes it to Caesarea, but it's probably your job if he doesn't make it. Clear? Good.''

He made sure that everything was clear, and in between continuing his dictation to the Governor finished up the logistical directions.

``Take the day to plan and prepare. Then set off just before dark and head to Antipatris. You should get there tomorrow morning. Leave the prisoner in the barracks there and come back here. I'll send covering orders for the garrison there to take him the rest of the way. Clear?''

And when the scribe had finished the dictation to the governor he dictated his second message to the Colonel in Antipatris.

Within less than an hour everything was in readiness and I went back to see Paul in his cell.

``They are going to send you to the Governor in Caesarea with a recommendation to release you there. Hopefully we will be able to sail directly from there back to Asia or Europe. There doesn't seem to be a case against you that will stand up in any Roman court.''

``It would be nice to think so,'' responded Paul, ``but I don't think so. The prophecies and visions have been quite clear. I am to be taken to Rome in chains. If I leave Caesarea by ship it will be as a prisoner. But if I can testify in front of both the governor here and the Consuls in Rome it is a small price to pay for being a prisoner - even to be executed! Rome is an apple rotten at its core with maggots and ready for repentance, redemption and salvation.''

``And you are the man to bring it to them,'' I said, mildly challenging.

``Who else?'' he responded, tartly. ``I am appointed missionary to the Gentiles. And who could argue that the Roman consuls are anything except Gentiles and chiefs of the Gentiles? It is to them that I must go and I must preach to them.''

And from that position he would not be budged.

Because of the haste and secrecy of the mission, I and the others of Paul's companions were not allowed to go with him - and indeed, were not allowed out of the prison until after Paul had left that evening.

Loath as I was to be parted from Paul, I recognized that this was the safest strategy and said good-bye to him as he left. He made it to Caesarea early in the morning of the following day, having spent a day sleeping in Antipatris.

I made my way separately back to Caesarea at a more sedate pace with Timothy, Trophimus and some of the others. Titus wanted to stay longer with the brothers at Jerusalem and said there was no hurry - Roman justice moved slowly and it was unlikely that Paul's case would be heard, let alone decided, for months.

It was over a week before I would see Paul again.
